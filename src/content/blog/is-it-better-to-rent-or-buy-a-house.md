## Is it better to rent or buy a house? The short answer

**It is better to buy a house when you will stay long enough to recover the costs of buying and selling (usually 5–10 years), when rents are high compared with prices, and when you value stability. It is better to rent when homes are expensive relative to rents, when mortgage rates are high compared with what your savings could earn, or when you might move soon.** Neither choice is "throwing money away". Both have costs you never get back, and the right answer comes from comparing those costs honestly.

That is the whole decision in two sentences. The rest of this guide shows you how to put your own numbers into it, how the answer differs between countries, and the mistakes that push people to the wrong conclusion.

## Why "rent is dead money" is the wrong starting point

Most people answer the question by comparing their rent with the mortgage payment on a similar home. If the mortgage is close to the rent, buying looks obvious. That comparison misleads in two directions.

First, part of a mortgage payment is not a cost at all. The principal portion is savings: it reduces your debt and increases your equity. Second, a large part of what homeowners pay is not in the mortgage payment. Property tax, insurance, maintenance, strata or service charges, buying costs and selling costs are all real money that never comes back.

The fair comparison is between **unrecoverable costs**:

- **Renter's unrecoverable costs:** rent, renter's insurance and any renter fees.
- **Owner's unrecoverable costs:** mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance, service charges, buying and selling costs, and the return the down payment would have earned if it had been invested instead (the opportunity cost).

Against these, the owner gets home price appreciation. The renter gets the investment returns on the money they did not put into a house. If you want the full framework, our guide to [the cost of owning a home vs renting](/blog/hidden-costs-of-owning-a-home/) lists every cost line by country.

## The five numbers that decide whether renting or buying is better

When you strip the decision down, five inputs do almost all the work.

### 1. Price-to-rent ratio

The price-to-rent ratio is the home price divided by one year of rent for a comparable home. As a common rule of thumb, a ratio below about 15 tends to favour buying and one above about 20 tends to favour renting. Using our calculator's default figures, a typical US home (US$420,000, renting for US$2,100 a month) has a ratio of about 16.7, while Sydney (A$1.45 million vs A$3,460 a month) is around 35 and Toronto around 32. Read more in our explainer on the [price-to-rent ratio](/blog/price-to-rent-ratio/).

### 2. How long you will stay

Buying costs (stamp duty, transfer tax, legal fees) and selling costs (agent commission) are paid once, however long you stay. Stay two years and they swamp everything else. Stay fifteen and they are spread thin. This is why the break-even year is so useful. Our guide to the [rent vs buy break-even point](/blog/rent-vs-buy-break-even-point/) explains how to find yours.

### 3. Mortgage rate vs investment return

If your mortgage costs 6–7% and a diversified portfolio is expected to return a similar amount, every dollar of equity in the house earns roughly what it would earn elsewhere. If mortgage rates are low and expected investment returns are high, the renter's invested savings grow faster. As of early November 2026, Freddie Mac's [Primary Mortgage Market Survey](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms) put the average US 30-year fixed rate at about 7.2%, higher than the 6.3% default in our calculator. That alone tilts the US math further toward renting.

### 4. Home price growth vs rent growth

Appreciation is the owner's main financial reward. Rent growth is the renter's main risk. A home that appreciates 3.5% a year doubles in about 20 years. If rents grow faster than prices, renting gets more expensive over time and buying looks better.

### 5. Recurring ownership costs

Property tax ranges from zero (the UK, where council tax is paid by renters too, and Dubai) to more than 2% a year in some US states. Maintenance typically runs around 1% of the home's value per year. These recurring costs compound with the home's value, so they matter more than most buyers expect.

Read the break-even rent column like this: **if you can rent a comparable home for less than this amount, renting comes out ahead over 10 years; if rent is higher, buying wins.** In the US, renting at US$2,100 beats buying because it is below the ~US$2,310 break-even rent. In the UK, rent of £1,350 is far above the ~£800 break-even, so buying wins comfortably.

Three patterns stand out:

- **High-yield markets favour buying.** Where annual rent is 5.5–6.5% of the price (UK, Ireland, Dubai), buying wins within a few years.
- **Expensive markets favour renting.** Where rent is 3–4% of the price (Toronto, Sydney, Indian metros), the owner's interest and costs exceed rent by a wide margin.
- **The US is close to the line.** Small changes, such as a rent of US$2,400 instead of US$2,100, flip the US result to buying, with a break-even around year eight.

## Worked example: is it better financially to rent or buy a house in the US?

Take the US defaults: a US$420,000 home, 20% down (US$84,000), 3% buying costs (US$12,600), and a US$336,000 mortgage at 6.3% over 30 years.

- **Monthly mortgage payment:** about US$2,080. In month one, about US$1,764 of that is interest and only US$316 is principal.
- **Other owner costs in month one:** property tax (1.1%) about US$385, maintenance (1%) US$350 and insurance (0.55%) about US$193. That adds about US$928.
- **Total buyer outflow in month one:** about US$3,007, against rent of US$2,100 plus US$15 renter's insurance.
- **What the renter does:** invests the US$96,600 the buyer spent upfront, plus the roughly US$900 a month difference, at a 7% return.

After 10 years, assuming 3.5% annual home price growth and 3.5% rent growth, the buyer's net worth (home equity after 6% selling costs) is about US$273,500. The renter's portfolio is about US$315,000. **The renter is ahead by around US$42,000.**

Change one assumption and the picture moves:

| Change to US defaults | 10-year result |
|---|---|
| Rent is US$2,400, not US$2,100 | Buyer ahead by ~US$17,000 |
| Home prices grow 4.5% a year | Buyer ahead by ~US$6,000 |
| Investments return 5%, not 7% | Buyer ahead by ~US$4,000 |
| Mortgage rate 7.3% (near late-2026 market rates) | Renter ahead by ~US$87,000 |
| Maintenance 1.5% a year (older home) | Renter ahead by ~US$77,000 |

That sensitivity is the real lesson. In a balanced market like the US, the answer depends on your local rent, your rate and how much the house will cost to maintain. You can test these on the [US rent vs buy calculator](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/united-states/).

## When is buying a house better than renting?

Buying tends to be the better choice when most of these are true:

- You expect to stay in the area for at least 7–10 years.
- Comparable homes rent for more than the break-even rent.
- Your mortgage rate is close to, or below, what you could realistically earn on investments after tax.
- You have an emergency fund on top of the down payment and buying costs.
- Your income is stable enough to handle repairs and rate changes.
- You know you would not consistently invest money that you do not spend on housing.

That last point is underrated. A mortgage forces saving. Many renters intend to invest the difference but spend it instead. In the US example, if the renter invests only about two-thirds of the monthly savings, the buyer comes out ahead.

## When is renting a house better than buying?

Renting tends to be the better choice when:

- You might move for work, study or family within three to five years.
- Local prices are high relative to rents (a price-to-rent ratio above about 20).
- Mortgage rates are high and you can invest in a low-cost diversified portfolio.
- Buying costs are large, such as Germany's Kaufnebenkosten of around 10% or Spain's transfer taxes of roughly 6–10%.
- You would have to stretch to the maximum loan, leaving no buffer for repairs.
- The home you can afford to buy is a big compromise compared with the one you can rent.

## What about the non-financial side?

Money is not the only output. Owning gives security of tenure, freedom to renovate and keep pets, and protection from rent increases and landlord decisions. In countries where tenants have weaker protections, that security is worth a lot. Renting gives flexibility, a predictable cost ceiling for repairs, and the ability to move to a better neighbourhood or job without a sale. Our guide to [owning a home vs renting](/blog/owning-a-home-vs-renting/) covers these trade-offs in detail.

A sensible way to handle this is to work out the financial gap first. If buying costs you an extra US$40,000 over ten years, ask whether the stability is worth about US$330 a month to you. Sometimes it clearly is.

## A step-by-step way to get your own answer

1. **Find a real comparable.** Look up the price of a home you would buy and the rent on a similar one in the same area.
2. **Set an honest horizon.** How long will you realistically live there? Pick the shorter end if unsure.
3. **Use local costs.** Enter your mortgage rate, property tax, buying costs and selling costs, not national averages.
4. **Choose a realistic investment return.** Use an after-tax return for a portfolio you would actually hold.
5. **Check the break-even rent and break-even year.** These two numbers summarise the decision.
6. **Stress-test.** Lower appreciation by one point, raise the rate by one point and see if the answer flips.
7. **Add the non-financial value.** Decide what stability or flexibility is worth to you per month.

Our [methodology page](/methodology/) explains exactly how the calculator models each step. It does not model tax deductions, such as US mortgage interest, which only help if you itemize above the 2026 standard deduction of US$16,100 for single filers and US$32,200 for married couples filing jointly, according to the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-releases-tax-inflation-adjustments-for-tax-year-2026-including-amendments-from-the-one-big-beautiful-bill).

## Common mistakes that skew the rent vs buy answer

- **Comparing rent with principal and interest only.** Leaving out tax, insurance and upkeep can understate monthly ownership costs by 20–45% (about 45% with the US defaults).
- **Assuming past price growth continues.** Use a modest long-run rate, roughly in line with inflation plus a little.
- **Ignoring selling costs.** A 5–6% commission on a future sale price is a large cheque.
- **Forgetting the down payment has a cost.** Money in bricks is money not compounding in markets.
- **Using a 30-year horizon by default.** Most people move sooner than they think.
