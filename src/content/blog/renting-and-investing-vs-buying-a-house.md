## Renting and investing vs buying a house: which wins?

**Renting and investing beats buying a house when local rents are low compared with prices, your expected investment return is higher than your mortgage rate, and you actually invest the down payment plus every month's savings. It loses when rents are high relative to prices, or when the renter spends the difference instead of investing it.** On paper, renting and investing vs buying a house often looks like an easy win for the renter; in practice, discipline decides it.

This guide tests the "rent and invest vs buy" idea against four real-world problems: the discipline problem, forced savings, leverage and sequence risk. All figures use our calculator's late-2026 default assumptions.

## How the rent and invest strategy works

The renter does two things:

1. **Invests the lump sum** the buyer would have spent upfront: the down payment and buying costs.
2. **Invests the monthly difference** whenever owning would cost more than renting. If renting ever costs more (because rent has risen), the buyer invests that difference instead.

That makes it a fair comparison: both households spend the same on housing plus saving each month. Our [rent and invest vs buy calculator](/rent-and-invest-vs-buy-calculator/) runs exactly this, month by month.

For the US default (a US$420,000 home, 20% down, 6.3% mortgage, 7% investment return), the renter invests US$96,600 on day one and about US$900 a month at first. After ten years:

| Household | Net worth after 10 years |
|---|---|
| Buyer (home equity after selling costs) | ~US$273,500 |
| Renter who invests everything | ~US$315,300 |
| **Difference** | **Renter ahead by ~US$42,000** |

## Where it wins and where it doesn't

The strategy depends heavily on the market. Using each market's defaults and full discipline:

| Market | 10-year result | Why |
|---|---|---|
| India | Renter ahead ~₹13 lakh | Rent is ~4.5% of price; 10% expected returns vs 8.5% mortgage |
| Toronto | Renter ahead ~C$364,000 | Rent is ~3.1% of price |
| Canada | Renter ahead ~C$98,000 | Rent is ~3.9% of price |
| Germany | Renter ahead ~€65,000 | 10% buying costs; modest price growth |
| United States | Renter ahead ~US$42,000 | Close to balanced |
| Australia | Buyer ahead ~A$17,000 | 5% price growth with leverage |
| United Kingdom | Buyer ahead ~£106,000 | Rent close to mortgage; no property tax |
| UAE (Dubai) | Buyer ahead ~AED 940,000 | Rent above the mortgage payment |

In the UK and Dubai, rent-and-invest loses even with perfect discipline, because there is little or nothing to invest each month: the buyer's costs are close to, or below, the rent. ## Problem 1: the discipline problem

The comparison assumes the renter invests every spare dollar, every month, for years. Many people intend to but do not. Raises get absorbed into lifestyle, holidays get booked, and the monthly transfer quietly stops.

Here is what partial discipline does to the US example (the renter always invests the lump sum, but only part of the monthly difference):

| Share of monthly savings invested | Renter after 10 years | Buyer after 10 years | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100% | ~US$315,300 | ~US$273,500 | Renter |
| 75% | ~US$283,900 | ~US$273,500 | Renter, narrowly |
| 67% | ~US$273,500 | ~US$273,500 | Tie |
| 50% | ~US$252,600 | ~US$273,500 | Buyer |
| 0% | ~US$190,000 | ~US$273,500 | Buyer |

If you invest less than about two-thirds of the monthly difference, buying wins. In India the tipping point is higher, about 76% of the monthly savings, because most of the Indian renter's advantage comes from investing a large monthly gap (an EMI plus costs of about ₹62,900 against ₹30,000 rent) rather than from the lump sum.

**The fix:** automate it. Set up a standing transfer into an investment account on the day after payday, for the amount the calculator shows. Increase it each year as rent and costs change.

## Problem 2: forced savings work in the buyer's favour

A mortgage is a commitment device. Each payment includes principal, so the buyer saves without choosing to. In the US example, about US$52,600 of the loan is repaid in ten years, and the buyer cannot easily spend it.

That has real value for people who find saving hard. It also has a cost: equity in a house is illiquid. If you lose your job, you cannot sell a bedroom. The renter's portfolio can be drawn on, which is both its advantage and its weakness.

## Problem 3: leverage cuts both ways

A buyer with a 20% deposit controls an asset five times the size of their cash. In the US example, the home rises from US$420,000 to about US$592,000 in ten years at 3.5% a year. That US$172,000 gain comes on an initial US$96,600 of cash.

The renter's portfolio is usually unleveraged, so a 7% return is just 7%. This is why the result is so sensitive to house price growth:

- **Australia:** buyer ahead by about A$17,000 at 5% annual price growth, but behind by about A$103,000 at 4%.
- **United States:** at 4.5% growth instead of 3.5%, the buyer moves ahead by about US$6,000.

And the renter's result is sensitive to returns:

| Renter's annual return (US) | Renter after 10 years | vs buyer (~US$273,500) |
|---|---|---|
| 4% | ~US$248,800 | Buyer ahead |
| 5% | ~US$269,300 | Buyer ahead, narrowly |
| 6% | ~US$291,400 | Renter ahead |
| 7% | ~US$315,300 | Renter ahead |
| 9% | ~US$368,900 | Renter well ahead |

The break-even return in this example is just above 5% after tax. That is achievable with a stock-heavy portfolio over long periods, but not guaranteed, and not with cash savings.

## Problem 4: sequence risk

Averages hide timing. A calculator assumes a steady return every year, but markets do not behave that way. A crash right before you need the money hurts much more than one at the start, because by then the portfolio is larger.

Using the US example, suppose the renter earns 7% every year except one year of −35%:

| Scenario | Renter after 10 years |
|---|---|
| Steady 7% every year | ~US$315,300 |
| −35% in year 1, then 7% | ~US$236,600 |
| 7% for nine years, then −35% in year 10 | ~US$192,700 |

In both crash cases, the buyer (about US$273,500, assuming steady house prices) comes out ahead. House prices can fall too, of course, and a forced sale in a slump hurts owners just as badly. But a renter who needs their portfolio to fund a future home purchase or retirement should reduce stock risk as that date approaches.

## How to make rent and invest work

1. **Check the market first.** If rent is above the break-even rent, buying likely wins regardless. See [the rent vs buy break-even point](/blog/rent-vs-buy-break-even-point/).
2. **Invest the lump sum promptly** in a diversified, low-cost portfolio suited to your timeline.
3. **Automate the monthly difference** and review it every year.
4. **Use tax-advantaged accounts** where you can; our calculator assumes an after-tax return.
5. **Keep a separate emergency fund**, so you do not sell investments in a downturn.
6. **Plan for rent in retirement,** when the owner's costs fall but yours do not.

## Buying a home vs renting and investing: the verdict

Renting and investing is a strong strategy in expensive, low-yield markets like Toronto, Sydney and Indian metros, and a marginal one in balanced markets like the US. It rarely wins where rents are close to or above mortgage payments, such as the UK, Dubai and Ireland. Above all, it only works for people who invest consistently. If that is not you, the forced savings of a mortgage may be worth more than the theoretical edge.
