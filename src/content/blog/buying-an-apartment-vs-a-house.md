## Is it better to buy an apartment or a house?

When you weigh buying apartment vs house options, the apartment usually wins on entry price, location and day-to-day upkeep, and the house usually wins on long-run growth, space and control. **The core difference is land: a house's price is largely land, which tends to appreciate, while an apartment's price is largely building, which depreciates and comes with rising shared fees.** Which is better depends on your budget, how long you will stay and the specific building.

There is no universal winner, and both can lose to renting if the numbers are wrong. Below we break down the costs, run worked examples, and give a checklist for deciding.

## How do the costs of an apartment and a house differ?

The purchase price is only the first line. Ownership costs are structured differently.

| Cost | Apartment / flat / condo | House |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase price | Usually lower for the same location | Usually higher |
| Transaction costs | Same percentage, smaller amount | Same percentage, larger amount |
| Building fees (HOA, strata, service charge) | Monthly, typically rise each year | Usually none (some estates charge) |
| Maintenance | Lower; exterior and structure shared via fees | Higher; roof, garden, exterior all yours |
| Insurance | Often building cover inside the fee; you insure contents | Full building insurance |
| Special levies | Possible for major repairs | No, but you pay for big repairs directly |
| Property tax | Lower in value-based systems | Higher in value-based systems |
| Control over changes | Limited by building rules | Broad, subject to planning |

A useful number is the **fee-to-price ratio**: annual building fees divided by the purchase price. An apartment costing $320,000 with $400 a month in HOA fees has a fee ratio of 1.5% a year, on top of its own maintenance, insurance and property tax. That is in the same range as the 1% maintenance budget a house owner might set aside, so the "apartments are cheaper to run" story often does not survive the fee schedule.

## Apartment vs house as an investment

### Why land matters

A home's value has two parts: the land and the structure. Land tends to rise with population, incomes and limits on supply. Structures wear out and need replacing. A detached house on its own plot has a high land share of value. An apartment in a 20-storey tower shares one plot between many owners, so each owns a small slice of land and a large slice of ageing concrete.

Japan shows the extreme version: buildings are widely treated as depreciating assets, and land drives what a property is worth over time. Our [Japan defaults](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/) assume just 1% annual price growth for a Tokyo resale condo for this reason.

### Yield vs growth

Apartments often rent for more relative to their price, giving a higher **gross rental yield** (annual rent divided by price). Houses often deliver more **capital growth**. For a home you live in, yield matters indirectly: a high yield means renting the same apartment is relatively expensive, which favours buying it.

## Worked examples

These use our calculator engine with a 10-year horizon. They are illustrations; local numbers will differ.

### United States: $420,000 house vs $320,000 apartment

The house uses our [United States defaults](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/united-states/): $420,000 price, $2,100 rent, 6.3% rate, 20% down, 1% maintenance and 3.5% annual growth. The apartment costs $320,000, rents for $1,800, has $400 monthly HOA fees, 0.5% maintenance, and is assumed to grow 3% a year.

| 10-year result | House | Apartment |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly mortgage payment | $2,080 | $1,585 |
| First-month cost to own (all-in) | $3,007 | $2,491 |
| Rent for a comparable home | $2,100 | $1,800 |
| Buyer net worth after 10 years | $273,500 | $188,300 |
| Renter net worth after 10 years | $314,900 | $236,300 |
| Verdict | Rent by about $41,400 | Rent by about $48,000 |
| Break-even rent | $2,310 | $2,044 |

At these assumptions, renting comes out ahead for both at today's rates, but the apartment is the weaker purchase despite its lower price. If the apartment only grows 2% a year, renting's lead widens to about $81,200. The [rent vs buy condo calculator](/rent-vs-buy-condo-calculator/) is set up to test these fee and growth assumptions, and our [condo vs renting guide](/blog/buying-a-condo-vs-renting/) goes deeper on HOA risk.

### Sydney: house vs unit

Our Sydney default is a A$1.45 million house renting for about A$800 a week (A$3,460 a month), a gross yield of just 2.9%. Compare an illustrative A$850,000 unit renting for about A$700 a week (A$3,030 a month) with A$450 a month in strata levies and 3.5% growth.

The unit's gross yield is 4.3%, and its monthly mortgage is about A$3,900 versus A$6,660 for the house. Over 10 years at 5.6% rates, renting beats buying the house by about A$102,000 and beats buying the unit by about A$75,000. The unit is the less-bad purchase because its rent is high relative to price. If houses outgrow units by more than the default gap, the house gains ground over longer horizons. Try it in the [Australia calculator](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/australia/).

### London: leasehold flats and service charges

Most London flats are leasehold, with service charges and sometimes ground rent. With our London defaults (a £550,000 flat, £2,300 rent, £150 a month service charge), buying beats renting by about £98,600 over 10 years. Raise the service charge to £300 a month and the lead falls to about £69,900; cut price growth to 1.5% a year and it falls to about £37,500. In the UK, the Leasehold Reform (Ground Rent) Act 2022 set ground rent on most new residential long leases to a peppercorn, and a draft Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill published in January 2026 proposes capping existing ground rents at £250 a year. It is not yet law.

### What about outside these three markets?

The same forces show up elsewhere with local twists. In Dubai, most buyers purchase apartments, service charges are set per square foot and published through the Dubai Land Department, and villas carry higher prices but often lower charges relative to value. In Singapore, private condominiums come with monthly maintenance fees and shared facilities, while public HDB flats follow entirely different rules on eligibility and resale. In Germany and Spain, apartment owners belong to a community of owners that sets fees and approves major works. Wherever you buy, read the building's rules and finances as carefully as the property itself.

## Buying a house vs buying an apartment: who should choose which?

### An apartment tends to suit you if

- You want a central location your budget could not reach with a house.
- You will likely move within 5–7 years; a lower price means lower buying and selling costs in absolute terms.
- You do not want to manage a roof, garden or exterior.
- The building has healthy reserves and a stable fee history.
- You value amenities such as security, a gym or concierge.

### A house tends to suit you if

- You plan to stay 10 years or more, so land-driven growth has time to work.
- You need space for a growing household or a home office.
- You want control over renovations, extensions, pets and parking.
- You have savings to handle irregular big repairs.
- Your market has a history of houses outgrowing apartments.

## Checklist before you buy either

1. **Price the whole cost:** mortgage, fees, maintenance, insurance and property tax per month.
2. **For apartments, read the building's books:** reserve fund, planned works, fee increases over five years, any litigation or cladding issues.
3. **Check the tenure:** freehold, leasehold (and the years remaining), strata title or co-op, and what each allows.
4. **Compare with renting the same home,** not a different one. Use our [price-to-rent ratio guide](/blog/price-to-rent-ratio/) to sanity-check value.
5. **Set a horizon** and run the numbers at lower growth, because apartments' growth is the assumption most likely to disappoint.
6. **Think about resale:** who will buy this home from you, and what will competing new supply look like?
