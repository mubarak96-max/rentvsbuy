## Buying a condo vs renting: the short answer

**Buying a condo is better than renting when the monthly association fee is low relative to the rent you would pay, the building's finances are healthy, and you will stay long enough to absorb buying and selling costs, usually five years or more.** When fees are high, or reserves are thin, renting a comparable unit is often the cheaper and less risky option. The fee is the single number most likely to flip the result.

Condos are called different things around the world: strata title in Australia and British Columbia, condominium or co-op in North America, leasehold flats with service charges in the UK, and community property in Spain. The rent-versus-buy logic is the same everywhere. You own your unit plus a share of a building you do not control.

## Why condos are different from houses in a rent vs buy comparison

When you compare renting versus buying a condo, three things set it apart from a house:

1. **A mandatory monthly fee.** HOA dues, strata fees or service charges cover building insurance, common-area upkeep, staff, amenities and contributions to a reserve fund. They are not optional and usually rise each year.
2. **Shared repair risk.** Big-ticket items such as roofs, lifts, facades and plumbing risers are funded collectively. If reserves fall short, owners face special assessments.
3. **Different price dynamics.** Apartments sit on less land per unit, and land tends to drive long-term appreciation. In many cities condo prices have grown more slowly than house prices, and new supply can arrive quickly.

On the plus side, your own maintenance spend is usually lower than for a house, because the association handles the exterior.

## Should I buy a condo or rent? A worked US example

We modelled a $350,000 US condo against renting a similar unit for $2,000 a month. Inputs: 20% down, 6.3% 30-year mortgage, 1.1% property tax, 0.5% maintenance (lower than a house, since the HOA covers the exterior), 0.25% insurance (contents and interior), 3% price growth, 3.5% rent growth, 7% investment return and a 10-year horizon. The calculator grows the HOA fee in line with rent.

| Monthly HOA fee | Owner's first-month cost | Renter's first-month cost | Result after 10 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0 | $2,273 | $2,015 | Buy ahead by ~$42,000 |
| $300 | $2,573 | $2,015 | Rent ahead by ~$17,000 |
| $450 | $2,723 | $2,015 | Rent ahead by ~$46,000 |
| $600 | $2,873 | $2,015 | Rent ahead by ~$76,000 |
| $900 | $3,173 | $2,015 | Rent ahead by ~$135,000 |

Every $150 of monthly fee moved the ten-year result by roughly $30,000. That is because the fee is paid for 120 months, rises over time, and the renter invests the difference.

At a $450 fee, the break-even rent is about **$2,235**: if a comparable unit rents for less than that, renting wins on these assumptions. Holding the fee at $450, the result also depends heavily on price growth. At 2% annual appreciation the renter's lead grows to about $83,000. Over five years it is about $39,000; over twenty, about $60,000.

## Is it better to buy a condo or rent in other markets?

### Toronto

Toronto condo fees are significant. We tested a C$650,000 condo with C$600 monthly fees against a C$2,600 rent, using Toronto's other defaults (20% down, 4.4% mortgage, 2.5% price growth). The owner's first-month cost is about **C$4,138** versus **C$2,625** for the renter. After ten years the renter is about C$101,000 ahead. The break-even rent is around C$3,136. With a price-to-rent ratio near 21 and big monthly fees, renting holds a clear edge unless prices grow faster than assumed.

### Singapore

Our Singapore default represents a private condominium for a citizen buying a first home: S$1.5 million, S$4,500 rent, 25% down, a 2.8% mortgage and S$350 monthly maintenance fees. Despite a high price-to-rent ratio of 27.8, buying wins by about **S$143,000** over ten years and breaks even in year four. The low mortgage rate does most of the work. HDB flats have very different economics, so treat this as a private-condo example only. Try it in the [Singapore calculator](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/singapore/).

### London

London's default flat (£550,000, £2,300 rent, £150 monthly service charge) favours buying by about £99,000 over ten years. Doubling the service charge to £300 cuts that to about £70,000 and pushes the break-even year from four to five. Many London flats are leasehold, so ground rent and lease length also matter. See our [UK rent vs buy guide](/blog/rent-vs-buy-uk/) for more.

## Why condo fees matter so much more than they look

A condo fee feels small next to a mortgage payment, but it behaves differently. The mortgage on a fixed-rate loan stays flat; the fee rises, usually every year. Over ten years at 3.5% annual increases, a $450 monthly fee totals about $63,000. The principal you repay builds equity; none of the fee does.

Fees also cut into what you can borrow. Lenders count them alongside the mortgage, property tax and insurance when they assess affordability, so a high-fee building reduces the price you can qualify for.

Finally, fees are capitalised into prices. Buyers pay less for units in buildings with high or rising charges, so a fee jump can hit your sale price as well as your budget. When you compare renting versus buying a condo, model the fee as a cost that grows like rent, not a fixed add-on, and stress-test a few years of above-inflation increases.

## Renting or buying a condo: what to check before you decide

The fee number in your calculation is only as good as the building behind it. Before you buy, review:

- **Fee history.** Steady annual increases are normal. Sudden jumps can signal deferred maintenance.
- **Reserve fund and reserve study.** Is there a recent study (called a depreciation report in British Columbia) showing upcoming major repairs and whether reserves cover them?
- **Special assessments.** Ask about past, current and proposed assessments.
- **Insurance.** What does the master policy cover, and what is the deductible owners might share?
- **Litigation.** Lawsuits against developers or contractors can tie up money for years.
- **Rental and pet rules.** These affect your flexibility and resale value.
- **Owner-occupancy ratio.** Buildings with many investor-owned units can be harder to finance.

## Buy a condo or rent an apartment: who should choose which?

| Situation | Leans toward | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Staying 7+ years, low fees, healthy reserves | Buy | Costs spread over time; equity builds |
| Fee above ~20% of comparable rent | Rent | Fee erodes the buying advantage quickly |
| Likely to move within 3–5 years | Rent | Transaction costs dominate |
| Older building with thin reserves | Rent | Assessment risk falls on owners |
| Low mortgage rate market (e.g. Singapore defaults) | Buy | Cheap financing outweighs high price-to-rent |
| You want to rent it out later and bylaws allow it | Buy | Flexibility to keep it as an investment |

## How to compare renting versus buying a condo in five steps

1. Find a comparable rental in the same or a similar building, with the same size and amenities.
2. Get the exact monthly fee and the last three years of increases.
3. Enter the fee in the calculator's HOA field and set maintenance lower than you would for a house (0.3–0.6% is a reasonable starting range).
4. Use a cautious appreciation rate. If local condo prices have lagged houses, reflect that.
5. Read the break-even rent. If comparable rentals are cheaper, renting is the better deal on your assumptions.

## The bottom line

Buying a condo vs renting comes down to the fee and the building more than the condo itself. A well-run building with modest charges can make ownership clearly better over a long stay. A building with high or fast-rising fees, or one heading for a big assessment, can make renting the smarter financial choice even in a market where buying houses makes sense.
