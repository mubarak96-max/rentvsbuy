## Is it better to pay your mortgage monthly or bi-weekly?

On a mortgage payment monthly vs bi weekly comparison, **accelerated biweekly payments win because they add one extra monthly payment each year**: 26 half-payments equal 13 monthly payments instead of 12. On a $336,000, 30-year loan at 6.3%, that **saves about $92,000 in interest and pays the loan off about 5.7 years early**. A "plain" biweekly schedule that splits the same yearly total into 26 pieces saves only around $1,000.

The words sound alike, but the two schedules are completely different products. Before you sign up for anything, it is worth knowing which one you are getting and whether you are paying a fee for it.

## Accelerated biweekly vs plain biweekly: the definitions

**Accelerated biweekly** means paying exactly half your monthly payment every two weeks. There are 52 weeks in a year, so you make 26 payments, which add up to 13 full monthly payments. The 13th payment goes straight to principal.

**Plain (non-accelerated) biweekly** means taking your annual total (12 monthly payments) and dividing it by 26. Each payment is smaller than half a monthly payment, and you pay the same total per year as before. You save only the tiny benefit of paying slightly earlier.

The same logic applies to **weekly** schedules: a quarter of the monthly payment every week is "accelerated weekly" (13 months' worth a year); the annual total divided by 52 is plain weekly.

## How much does each option save? A worked example

We modelled the loan from our [United States calculator](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/united-states/) defaults: a $420,000 home with 20% down, so a $336,000 loan at 6.3% over 30 years. The standard monthly payment is $2,079.75, and total interest over the full term is about $412,700. Interest was charged on each payment period at the annual rate divided by the number of periods.

| Payment schedule | Payment | Paid per year | Time to pay off | Total interest | Interest saved | Time saved |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly (standard) | $2,079.75 | $24,957 | 30.0 years | $412,700 | — | — |
| Plain biweekly (÷26) | $959.88 | $24,957 | 29.96 years | $411,700 | about $1,050 | about 2 weeks |
| Plain weekly (÷52) | $479.94 | $24,957 | 29.94 years | $411,200 | about $1,500 | about 3 weeks |
| Accelerated biweekly (½ monthly) | $1,039.87 | $27,037 | 24.3 years | $320,400 | about $92,400 | about 5.7 years |
| Accelerated weekly (¼ monthly) | $519.94 | $27,037 | 24.3 years | $320,000 | about $92,800 | about 5.7 years |
| Monthly + 1/12 extra | $2,253.06 | $27,037 | 24.3 years | $321,300 | about $91,400 | about 5.7 years |

Three things stand out:

1. **Frequency on its own is almost worthless.** Plain biweekly and plain weekly save around $1,000–$1,500 over 30 years.
2. **The extra payment does nearly all the work.** Paying monthly with one-twelfth extra saves 99% of what accelerated biweekly saves.
3. **Weekly and biweekly accelerated are effectively identical** once the annual total is the same.

At higher rates the savings grow. With the same loan at 7.2%, close to Freddie Mac's survey average for a 30-year fixed in early November 2026, accelerated biweekly saves about $123,000 and cuts about 6.4 years.

### Why the timing effect is so small

It can feel as though paying every two weeks should cut interest sharply, because the balance falls sooner. In practice, each half-payment arrives only about two weeks earlier than it otherwise would, so the interest avoided per payment is tiny: on a $336,000 balance at 6.3%, two weeks of interest on a $960 payment is under $3. Over 30 years that adds up to roughly the $1,000 shown in the table. The extra 13th payment, by contrast, removes about $2,080 of principal every year, and every dollar of principal removed stops generating interest for all the remaining years of the loan. That compounding is why the accelerated version saves around 90 times more than the plain version.

### How to use a weekly vs monthly mortgage calculator correctly

If you use a weekly vs monthly mortgage calculator, check which payment it assumes. Many show the accelerated amount by default without labelling it, which makes frequency look powerful. Enter the same annual total in each schedule and the gap almost disappears.

## Fortnightly vs monthly repayments in Australia and New Zealand

Australian and New Zealand lenders commonly let you choose weekly, fortnightly or monthly repayments, and rents there are quoted weekly too. The same distinction applies: what matters is whether your fortnightly amount is half the monthly amount or the monthly total spread over 26 fortnights.

| Loan (30 years) | Monthly repayment | Fortnightly at half of monthly | Interest saved | Years saved |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Australia: A$720,000 at 5.6% | A$4,133 | A$2,067 | about A$155,000 | about 5.1 |
| New Zealand: NZ$640,000 at 5.0% | NZ$3,436 | NZ$1,718 | about NZ$110,000 | about 4.7 |

These loans match our [Australia](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/australia/) and [New Zealand](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/new-zealand/) default prices with 20% deposits. If your lender instead sets the fortnightly figure at the annual total divided by 26 (A$1,908 here), the saving drops to under A$2,000.

Two local features matter more than frequency:

- **Offset accounts.** Money held in an offset account linked to your loan reduces the balance interest is charged on. Paying your salary into one can save more than changing frequency.
- **Redraw.** Many variable loans let you take extra repayments back out, which gives flexibility but can tempt you to spend the savings.

## Accelerated biweekly mortgages in Canada

Canadian banks routinely offer **accelerated biweekly** and **accelerated weekly** options alongside regular biweekly, often at no charge. On a C$560,000 loan at 4.4% over a 25-year amortization (our [Canada](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/canada/) default with 20% down), the monthly payment is about C$3,081. Paying C$1,540 every two weeks cuts the amortization by about 3.3 years and saves roughly C$54,000 in interest.

Two caveats specific to Canada. First, fixed-rate Canadian mortgages compound interest semi-annually by law, so exact figures from your lender will differ slightly from our simplified calculation. Second, Canadian mortgages renew every one to five years, and you can usually change payment frequency at renewal.

## What about the UK?

UK lenders typically take one monthly payment, so the equivalent strategy is **overpaying**. Most fixed-rate deals allow overpayments of around 10% of the balance a year without early repayment charges. On our UK default loan of £261,000 at 4.3% over 25 years, overpaying by one-twelfth of the monthly payment (about £118 a month) saves about £23,900 in interest and about 3.2 years. Check whether your lender reduces the term or the payment after overpayments.

## Third-party biweekly programs and their fees

In the US, some servicers and independent companies sell biweekly payment programs. They may charge an enrolment fee, often a few hundred dollars, plus a fee on each debit. Some collect your half-payments but only pass them to the lender monthly, so you get no timing benefit at all. US regulators, including the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, have taken enforcement action against firms that misrepresented the savings and fees of these programs.

Before you enrol, ask:

1. Is there a setup fee or a per-payment fee?
2. Are payments credited to my loan when received, or held and forwarded monthly?
3. Can I get the same result by adding an extra principal payment myself?

For most borrowers, the simplest answer is **monthly payment + one-twelfth extra, marked as principal**, set up as a standing transfer. It costs nothing and saves almost exactly the same.

## Is paying faster always the right move?

Paying down a mortgage early gives a guaranteed return equal to your interest rate. Whether that beats investing depends on the rate and your situation:

- **High rate (6–8%):** extra payments are an attractive, risk-free return.
- **Low rate (1–3%):** investing the difference has historically been likely to earn more, though with more risk. The [renting and investing vs buying](/blog/renting-and-investing-vs-buying-a-house/) guide explores the same trade-off.
- **Emergency fund first:** money in a mortgage is hard to get back out quickly.
- **Check for prepayment penalties** and overpayment limits in your contract.
