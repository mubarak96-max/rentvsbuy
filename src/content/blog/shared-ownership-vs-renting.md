## Is shared ownership better than renting?

**Shared ownership is better than renting when you will stay at least several years, prices in the area rise at least modestly, and the service charge is reasonable. In those conditions you build equity in your share while renters build none, and our £300,000 example shows a 25% buyer about £33,000 ahead of a renter after ten years.** It is worse than renting when you may need to move soon, prices stall, or charges climb, because your monthly costs start higher and the home is harder to sell.

Shared ownership is a UK scheme, mainly in England, that lets you buy part of a home and rent the rest from a housing association or other landlord. This guide uses the current rules on GOV.UK and a worked example to compare it with renting.

## How shared ownership works in 2026

According to GOV.UK's [shared ownership guidance](https://www.gov.uk/shared-ownership-scheme):

- **Share size:** you buy between 10% and 75% of the home's full market value. The usual range is 25–75%; some homes allow 10%.
- **Eligibility:** household income must be **£80,000 or less**, or **£90,000 or less in London**, and you must be unable to afford a suitable home outright. First-time buyers qualify, as do some other groups.
- **Rent on the landlord's share:** for new-build homes, rent is limited to **3%** of the value of the share you do not own, and most landlords charge **2.75%**. It is reviewed yearly and cannot fall.
- **Rent increases:** for leases signed on or after 12 October 2023, the lease sets the formula, either RPI plus up to 0.5% or CPI plus 1%.
- **Leasehold:** all shared ownership homes are leasehold, and you usually pay service charges and sometimes ground rent.
- **Repairs:** you pay for repairs and maintenance whatever share you own. While you own less than 100%, there is usually an initial repair period of about 10 years in which the landlord covers essential structural and external repairs, and you can claim up to about £500 a year for certain fixtures.
- **Staircasing:** you can usually buy further shares of 10% or more. If you bought on or after 1 April 2021, you may be able to buy 1% a year for the first 15 years, priced using the House Price Index.

## Shared ownership vs renting: a worked example

We modelled a £300,000 flat in England. The alternative is renting a comparable flat at **£1,250 a month**. Assumptions for the shared owner: 10% deposit on the share, a 4.6% 25-year mortgage on the rest of the share, 1.5% buying costs on the share value, rent of 2.75% a year on the landlord's share, a £150 monthly service charge, 1% maintenance and 0.15% insurance on the full home value. Both rent and the shared owner's rent rise 4% a year; prices rise 3%; investments earn 6%; selling costs are 2%. The renter invests what the shared owner spent upfront plus any monthly difference.

### Month one, 25% share

| Cost | Shared owner (25%) | Renter |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront cash (deposit + fees) | £8,625 | £0 (invested instead) |
| Mortgage on £67,500 | £379 | – |
| Rent on 75% share (£225,000 × 2.75% ÷ 12) | £516 | £1,250 |
| Service charge | £150 | – |
| Maintenance (1% of full value) | £250 | – |
| Insurance | £38 | £10 |
| **Total monthly** | **£1,332** | **£1,260** |

The shared owner pays about £72 a month more at the start. The gain comes from owning 25% of a home that grows in value, plus about £1,470 of mortgage principal repaid in year one.

### After ten years, by share size

| Share bought | Upfront cash | First-month cost | Owner net worth | Renter net worth | Owner ahead by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25% | £8,625 | £1,332 | ~£52,600 | ~£19,800 | ~£32,800 |
| 40% | £13,800 | £1,456 | ~£79,400 | ~£43,300 | ~£36,100 |
| 75% | £25,875 | £1,746 | ~£148,700 | ~£105,000 | ~£43,700 |
| 100% (full purchase) | £34,500 | £1,954 | ~£198,200 | ~£149,100 | ~£49,100 |

A bigger share builds more equity but needs more cash and income. The renter's net worth rises with share size because the renter invests the bigger upfront sum and the larger monthly difference the owner pays.

### When the answer changes

We stress-tested the 25% case:

- **Sell after five years:** owner ahead by about £11,000, much less than at ten years.
- **Prices grow 1.5% instead of 3%:** owner ahead by about £22,900.
- **Mortgage at 5.5% instead of 4.6%:** owner ahead by about £25,500.

Shared ownership kept a lead in each test, but the lead shrank. Combine slow price growth, a short stay and rising service charges and renting could come out ahead. Our model also grows the shared owner's rent at the same rate as market rent; your lease formula may differ.

## The catches that spreadsheets miss

1. **Service charges.** You pay 100% of the building's charges even if you own 25%. New-build blocks can see sharp increases as warranties end. Ask for the five-year history and the budget.
2. **Rent only goes up.** The landlord's rent cannot fall, even if market rents do.
3. **Resale restrictions.** The housing provider usually gets first chance to find a buyer, and that buyer must be eligible. Sales can take longer.
4. **Staircasing costs.** Shares of 5% or more need an RICS valuation and may carry an admin fee of about £150–£500, plus legal fees. Buying more after prices have risen costs more.
5. **Mortgage choice.** Fewer lenders offer shared ownership mortgages, which can limit deals.
6. **Subletting.** Usually not allowed while you own less than 100%.

## Rent to Buy vs shared ownership

The two are often confused. Rent to Buy is a separate affordable-housing route in England in which a housing provider lets a home at no more than 80% of market rent, including service charges, for at least five years, according to the GOV.UK Capital Funding Guide. The idea is that you use the discount to save for a deposit.

| Feature | Shared ownership | Rent to Buy |
|---|---|---|
| Do you own anything? | Yes, your share | No, you're a tenant |
| Upfront cost | Deposit on share plus fees | Usual tenancy costs |
| Monthly cost | Mortgage + rent on unowned share + charges | Rent at up to 80% of market |
| Repairs | You pay (with initial support on newer leases) | Landlord, as with normal renting |
| Benefit from price rises | Yes, on your share | No |
| Exit | Sell your share, often via provider | Leave at end of tenancy, or buy |

Rent to Buy suits people who need time to save and want low risk. Shared ownership suits people who can afford a share now and want to start building equity. Some households use Rent to Buy first, then buy through shared ownership. For private rent-to-own deals, which are different again, see [rent to own vs mortgage](/blog/rent-to-own-vs-mortgage/).

## Renting vs shared ownership: who should choose which?

Shared ownership is likely better than renting if:

- You expect to stay five to ten years or more.
- You can afford the total monthly cost with a buffer, including rising service charges.
- You plan to staircase over time, ideally using the 1% option if your lease allows it.
- The local market has steady, if modest, price growth.

Renting is likely better if:

- You may relocate within a few years.
- Service charges are high or unpredictable.
- You would rather invest your deposit with full flexibility.
- You can save enough to buy outright within a few years anyway.

## The bottom line

Shared ownership vs renting is a trade: higher monthly costs and more restrictions in exchange for equity and protection from market rent rises on part of your home. Over a long stay with fair charges, the numbers usually favour shared ownership. Over a short stay, or in a block with steep service charges, renting can be the better deal.
