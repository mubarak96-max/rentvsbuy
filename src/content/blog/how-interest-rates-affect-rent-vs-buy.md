## How do interest rates affect the rent vs buy decision?

Here is how interest rates affect rent vs buy: they work by **changing the biggest unrecoverable cost of owning: mortgage interest**. Higher rates raise the monthly cost of buying, delay the break-even year and raise the rent at which buying starts to win. With our US defaults, buying breaks even in **year 4 at 3%, year 8 at 5%, and not within 30 years at 7%**. Lower rates do the reverse.

Rates matter more in some places than others. Where rents are high relative to prices, buying can still win at high rates. Where homes are expensive relative to rents, a couple of percentage points can flip the answer. That is why the rate you can actually get belongs at the centre of any rent vs buy calculation.

## Rent vs interest rate: the US example

We ran our [United States calculator](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/united-states/) defaults at different mortgage rates and changed nothing else: a $420,000 home, $2,100 rent, 20% down, 30-year term, 3.5% annual price and rent growth, and a 7% after-tax investment return, over 10 years.

| Mortgage rate | Monthly payment | 10-year result | Break-even year | Break-even rent (10 years) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3% | $1,417 | Buy ahead by about $100,000 | Year 4 | $1,592 |
| 4% | $1,604 | Buy ahead by about $58,700 | Year 5 | $1,802 |
| 5% | $1,804 | Buy ahead by about $15,900 | Year 8 | $2,019 |
| 6% | $2,014 | Rent ahead by about $28,000 | Year 26 | $2,242 |
| 7% | $2,235 | Rent ahead by about $72,900 | Not within 30 years | $2,470 |
| 8% | $2,465 | Rent ahead by about $118,700 | Not within 30 years | $2,703 |

**Break-even rent** is the monthly rent at which buying and renting finish level at the horizon. At 3%, renting only wins if you could rent a comparable home for under about $1,600. At 7%, renting wins unless comparable rent is above about $2,470, well above the $2,100 default.

For context, Freddie Mac's Primary Mortgage Market Survey put the average 30-year fixed rate at 7.18% in early November 2026 ([Freddie Mac PMMS](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms)). At that level, the US default scenario favours renting over a 10-year stay. Our calculator's default of 6.3% is an editable starting point; enter your own quote.

## Why the same rate rise matters less in some markets

The key is how rent compares with the price. We ran the same 3%, 5% and 7% test on our UK and Dubai defaults:

| Market (10-year horizon) | Gross rental yield | At 3% | At 5% | At 7% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| United States ($420,000 / $2,100) | 6.0% | Buy +$100,000, break-even year 4 | Buy +$15,900, year 8 | Rent +$72,900, never |
| United Kingdom (£290,000 / £1,350) | 5.6% | Buy +£145,300, year 2 | Buy +£84,800, year 2 | Buy +£20,700, year 7 |
| Dubai (AED 1.8M / AED 10,000) | 6.7% | Buy +AED 1.20M, year 2 | Buy +AED 870,000, year 3 | Buy +AED 516,000, year 4 |

Three forces explain the differences:

- **Rental yield.** Dubai's yield of about 6.7% means rent is expensive relative to price, so even a 7% mortgage leaves owning competitive.
- **Leverage and deposit size.** The UK default uses a 10% deposit. A small deposit means the renter has less money to invest, so a high rate hurts the buyer but the renter's head start is also small.
- **Investment return assumptions.** The US default assumes a 7% return for renters, the UK 6% and Dubai 6%. A high return for the renter amplifies the effect of high mortgage rates.

The [rent vs buy around the world](/blog/rent-vs-buy-around-the-world/) comparison shows this pattern across markets: the gap between rental yield and mortgage rate predicts most verdicts.

## Should I buy when interest rates are high?

High rates do not automatically mean "rent." They mean the home must be cheap enough relative to its rent, or your horizon long enough, for the higher interest to be recovered. Several factors can offset a high rate:

### Lower prices

When rates rise, prices often soften. In our US example at 7%, buying the same home for 10% less ($378,000) cuts the payment to about $2,012 and narrows renting's 10-year lead from about $72,900 to about $24,000, with buying pulling ahead in year 17.

### Lower investment returns

If you would realistically earn 5% rather than 7% on the money you would have invested, renting's advantage at 7% shrinks to about $24,400 over 10 years, and buying breaks even in year 15. Be honest about what return you would get. Our guide to the [opportunity cost of a down payment](/blog/opportunity-cost-of-a-down-payment/) shows how much this assumption matters.

### A long horizon

Over 30 years, the US default at 6% finally tips to buying, ahead by about $10,600. Longer stays give appreciation and rent growth more time to work. Our [break-even point guide](/blog/rent-vs-buy-break-even-point/) explains this time effect.

## "Marry the house, date the rate": does it work?

The idea is to buy the right home now at a high rate and refinance later when rates fall. It can work, but it relies on several things going right.

Here is the potential upside. A $336,000 loan at 7.2% has a payment of about $2,281. After three years the balance is about $325,400. Refinancing that at 5.5% over the remaining 27 years drops the payment to about $1,930, saving about $350 a month. If refinancing costs 2% of the loan (about $6,500), the saving pays for itself in under two years.

The risks:

1. **Rates may not fall,** or not by enough. Forecasts are unreliable; nobody can promise a refinance window.
2. **You must still qualify.** A lower income, a job change or a fall in the home's value can block a refinance.
3. **Costs eat into savings.** Closing costs, arrangement fees and, in some countries, early repayment charges reduce the gain.
4. **Prices may rise when rates fall,** as more buyers return. That helps existing owners, but it undermines the strategy for people who wait.
5. **It only exists in some systems.** US fixed-rate mortgages can usually be refinanced without a prepayment penalty. In many other countries, breaking a fixed rate early carries a charge.

The safe version is simple: **only buy if you can afford the payment at today's rate indefinitely**. Treat any future refinance as a bonus.

## Fixed vs variable: how rate risk differs by country

How exposed you are to rate changes depends on how mortgages work where you live.

| Market | Typical structure | Rate risk for owners |
|---|---|---|
| United States | 30-year fixed is standard; refinancing usually allowed without penalty | Low after purchase; you can refinance if rates fall |
| France | Fixed for the full term is common | Low; rate locked for decades |
| Germany | Fixed for 10–15 years, then refinance | Medium; one or two resets over a loan |
| United Kingdom | Two- or five-year fixes, then remortgage | Higher; resets every few years |
| Canada | Terms of one to five years within a 25-year amortization | Higher; regular renewals at market rates |
| Australia | Mostly variable, some short fixes | High; payments move with the cash rate |
| UAE | Short fixed period, then EIBOR-linked variable | High after the fixed period |
| Hong Kong | Usually HIBOR- or prime-linked | High; payments float with rates |

In reset-based systems, model a rate shock. On our UK default loan (£261,000 at 4.3%), the balance after a five-year fix is about £228,500; remortgaging at 6.3% raises the payment by about £256 a month. On our Canada default (C$560,000 at 4.4%), a five-year renewal at 6.4% adds about C$552 a month.

## How to stress-test your own decision

1. **Run the calculator at your quoted rate,** not an advertised headline rate.
2. **Rerun at 2 points higher** if your rate resets within the next five years.
3. **Check the break-even rent.** If comparable rent is close to it, small rate changes will flip your answer.
4. **Decide your horizon honestly.** High rates make short stays even less favourable to buying.
5. **Compare monthly costs** using our [rent vs mortgage calculator](/rent-vs-mortgage-calculator/), then look at the long-term picture in the main [rent vs buy calculator](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/).
