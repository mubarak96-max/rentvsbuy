## Why buying a house is better than renting

**Buying a house is better than renting when you will stay long enough to absorb buying and selling costs, when prices are moderate compared with local rents, and when your monthly payment is comfortably affordable.** Under those conditions, ownership turns a housing expense into a savings plan, amplifies price growth through leverage and shields you from rising rents. Remove any of those conditions and renting can come out ahead.

This page makes the strongest honest argument for owning. If you want the opposite view, we wrote that too: [is renting better than buying](/blog/is-renting-better-than-buying/). The aim of both is the same: help you see which conditions apply to you.

## Five reasons owning a home is better than renting

### 1. A mortgage forces you to save

Every mortgage payment includes some principal. On a standard amortising loan the principal share grows each month, so equity builds even if the home's price stays flat. On our US default ($420,000 home, $336,000 loan at 6.3% over 30 years), the payment is about $2,080 a month and roughly $316 of the first payment repays principal. By year ten the loan balance has fallen by about $53,000.

Behaviour is where this matters. Many financial comparisons assume the renter invests every spare dollar. Real households often do not. When we re-ran the US defaults with the renter's savings earning nothing, the buyer finished ten years about **$92,000 ahead**. With a 4% return, the buyer was still around $25,000 ahead. The mortgage enforces discipline that most people find hard to replicate.

### 2. Leverage multiplies price growth

With a mortgage, you control a whole home with a fraction of its price. If a £290,000 UK home rises 3% in a year, that is £8,700 of growth on a £29,000 (10%) deposit, a 30% gain on the cash you put in before interest and costs. Leverage works in reverse too, but over long periods in rising markets it is the main reason owners accumulate wealth.

### 3. Fixed costs while rents rise

A fixed-rate mortgage locks the largest part of an owner's housing cost. Rent does not stay fixed. At 4% annual rent growth, a £1,350 rent becomes about £2,000 within ten years. The owner's mortgage payment in a long-term fixed loan is the same in year ten as in year one. In markets where fixes are shorter (the UK, Canada, Australia), the payment can change at renewal, but it still tracks interest rates rather than local rents.

### 4. Lower housing costs in retirement

Once the mortgage is paid, an owner's housing cost drops to tax, insurance and upkeep. That is a powerful advantage on a fixed income. A renter needs savings large enough to fund rent that keeps rising for decades.

### 5. Security and control

Owners decide when to move, whether to renovate and whether to keep a dog. In markets with short leases and weak tenant protections, that security is worth something real, even if no spreadsheet captures it.

### What buying does not do

An honest case for owning also has to state its limits:

- **It does not make housing free.** In the US default, about $1,764 of the first $2,080 mortgage payment is interest. Add tax, maintenance and insurance and the owner's unrecoverable costs start near $2,690 a month, above the $2,100 rent. Equity comes from principal repayment and price growth, not from the payment itself.
- **It does not guarantee gains.** Leverage that turns 3% growth into a 30% return on the deposit turns a 10% price fall into a total loss of a 10% deposit.
- **It does not stay cheap to exit.** Selling costs of 2–7% are charged on the whole price, not on your equity.

None of these undo the reasons above. They explain why the benefits only show up under the right conditions, which is what the rest of this guide sets out.

## The conditions: when buying is better and when it is not

### Horizon

Buying and selling costs are paid once, so the longer you stay, the thinner they spread. In the UK defaults the buyer is ahead by about £13,000 after three years, £35,000 after five and £106,000 after ten. Our [rent vs buy break-even point guide](/blog/rent-vs-buy-break-even-point/) explains how to find your own crossover.

### Price relative to rent

Buying is strongest when homes cost less than roughly 15–20 times annual rent. Above about 25 times, renting and investing usually wins unless price growth is high. Our US default sits at 16.7 but still favours renting, because a 6.3% mortgage rate and 1.1% property tax raise ownership costs.

### Interest rates

The US case shows how much the rate matters. At 6.3%, buying trails over ten years. Our model needs one of these changes before buying wins in the US defaults:

- Price growth of 4.5% a year instead of 3.5%: buying edges ahead by about $6,000 at ten years and $61,000 at twenty.
- An investment return of 5% instead of 7%: buying is ahead by about $137,000 at twenty years.
- The renter not investing at all: buying is ahead by about $92,000 at ten years.

A lower mortgage rate alone, at 5.5%, nearly closes the gap (renting ahead by about $6,000 at ten years, with buying pulling ahead in year 12).

### Affordability

Buying only helps if the payment does not squeeze everything else. Lenders cap borrowing in various ways, as covered in our guide to [house price to income ratios](/blog/house-price-to-income-ratio/). A home that leaves no buffer for repairs or a job loss can turn a sound long-term plan into a forced sale.

## Is owning a home better than renting for you? A checklist

Buying is likely the better choice if most of these are true:

- You expect to stay at least five to seven years, ideally longer.
- The price is under about 20 times the annual rent of a comparable home.
- Your total ownership cost (payment, tax, insurance, maintenance, fees) is no more than about 30–35% of take-home pay.
- You have an emergency fund after paying the deposit and fees.
- You would not reliably invest the difference if you rented.
- You value stability, control and lower housing costs in retirement.

If several are false, run the numbers carefully before committing. Our [should I rent or buy guide](/blog/should-i-rent-or-buy/) walks through the decision step by step.

## The bottom line on buying vs renting

The best argument for buying is not that homes always go up. It is that ownership combines forced saving, leverage and cost certainty in one package, and most households benefit from all three over long periods. The argument holds in our UK, Irish, Dutch, Portuguese and Dubai defaults even when the renter invests every spare unit of currency. It weakens in high price-to-rent cities and for short stays. Check the conditions, then let your own numbers decide.
