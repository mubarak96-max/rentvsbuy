## Buying a house vs renting an apartment: what are you really comparing?

**Buying a house vs renting an apartment combines two decisions: whether to own or rent, and how much home to pay for. Because an apartment is usually smaller and cheaper, renting one almost always costs less each month and often leaves you wealthier, provided you invest the difference.** The fair way to decide is to price the space and the ownership choice separately, so you know what you are paying for.

Most rent-versus-buy tools assume the rented and bought homes are equivalent. When people ask "should I rent an apartment or buy a house", they are rarely comparing equivalent homes. A two-bedroom flat and a three-bedroom house with a garden are different products. That is fine, as long as you are clear that part of any cost gap is buying more space, not the act of owning.

## A two-step method for a fair comparison

### Step 1: Rent vs buy the same house

Compare buying the house with renting a similar house nearby. This isolates the pure ownership decision. Use the [rent vs buy house calculator](/rent-vs-buy-house-calculator/) with the house price, local property tax, maintenance and insurance, and the rent for a comparable house.

### Step 2: Price the space

Now swap in the apartment rent. The extra wealth the renter accumulates is the cost of the additional space and land over your horizon. Ask yourself whether that extra room, garden or quiet is worth that sum to you. If it is, the decision is really step 1. If it is not, renting the apartment wins regardless of step 1.

## Worked example: rent an apartment or buy a house in the US

Using our US defaults: a $420,000 house, 20% down, 6.3% 30-year mortgage, 1.1% property tax, 1% maintenance, 0.55% insurance, 3.5% annual price and rent growth, a 7% investment return and a 10-year horizon.

| Scenario | Monthly rent | Price-to-rent | Renter's lead after 10 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rent a comparable house | $2,100 | 16.7 | ~$41,000 |
| Rent a two-bed apartment | $1,700 | 20.6 | ~$120,000 |
| Rent a one-bed apartment | $1,500 | 23.3 | ~$159,000 |

In every row the buyer's net worth after ten years is the same, about $273,500. What changes is how much the renter can invest each month. The owner's first-month outflow is about $3,007. A $1,700 apartment renter spends roughly $1,715 including renter's insurance, leaving about $1,290 a month to invest, plus the $96,600 the buyer spent on the down payment and closing costs.

Reading the table with the two-step method:

- **Ownership choice:** renting a comparable house beats buying it by about $41,000. That is the step 1 answer on these assumptions.
- **Space choice:** moving from the comparable house to a two-bed apartment adds about $79,000 more to the renter's lead. That is roughly what the extra space would cost over ten years, including the investment growth you forgo.

The break-even rent for the house stays at about $2,310 whichever rental you look at, because it measures the comparable-home rent at which buying and renting tie.

## Owning a house vs renting an apartment in other markets

### Australia

Our Australia default house is A$900,000 against a comparable rent of A$2,800 a month (about A$650 a week). Buying the house edges ahead of renting it by about **A$17,000** after ten years, helped by a 5% default price growth assumption. Swap in a unit at A$2,300 a month and the renter pulls ahead by about **A$83,000**. In Australia, the house-versus-unit choice can matter more than the rent-versus-buy choice. ### United Kingdom

With our UK defaults, buying a £290,000 house beats renting a comparable one at £1,350 by about **£106,000** after ten years. Even against a £1,100 flat, buying the house is still about **£59,000** ahead. When the ownership case is strong, as it is with a 10% deposit and price-to-rent under 18, it survives a cheaper rental alternative. ## What a house costs that an apartment does not

The ongoing cost gap between a house and an apartment is bigger than the price gap suggests.

| Cost | Owning a house | Renting an apartment |
|---|---|---|
| Maintenance | ~1% of value a year; roof, boiler, exterior, garden | Landlord's responsibility |
| Insurance | Buildings and contents | Contents only |
| Property tax | Based on a higher value | Built into rent, if at all |
| Utilities | Larger space to heat and cool | Usually lower |
| Furnishing | More rooms to fill | Fewer |
| Commuting | Often further from the centre | Often closer |
| Moving cost | Agent fees, transfer taxes | Deposit, removal van |

Our guide to the [hidden costs of owning a home](/blog/hidden-costs-of-owning-a-home/) goes deeper on maintenance and repairs.

What the house gives back:

- **Land.** Land typically does most of the appreciating, and houses carry more of it per dollar than apartments.
- **Control.** You can extend, renovate or add a granny flat (subject to planning rules).
- **No shared-building risk.** No service-charge disputes or special assessments.

## Should I rent an apartment or buy a house? Decision guide

Rent an apartment if:

- You are not sure you will stay in the area for five or more years.
- You do not need the extra space now, and may not for a few years.
- You will invest the monthly savings automatically.
- Commute time or city-centre amenities matter more to you than a garden.

Buy a house if:

- You need the space for the long term, for children, work from home or relatives.
- The house's price-to-rent ratio is moderate (roughly under 20).
- You expect to stay at least seven to ten years.
- You can afford maintenance without dipping into your emergency fund.

Consider a middle path:

- **Buy an apartment** if you want ownership without house-level costs. See [buying an apartment vs a house](/blog/buying-an-apartment-vs-a-house/).
- **Rent a house** for a year in the neighbourhood you are considering before you buy.

## Life stage changes the answer

The same household can reach different answers at different points in life.

- **Early career, single or a couple without children:** an apartment's lower cost and flexibility usually win. Moving for a better-paid job can be worth more than a decade of house-price growth, and an apartment renter can relocate with a month or two of notice.
- **Growing family:** space needs rise and moves become disruptive. School catchments matter. If you expect to stay put for seven to ten years, buying the house, or at least renting a house, starts to make sense.
- **Empty nesters:** the extra rooms may no longer be needed. Selling a house and renting an apartment can release equity to invest or spend, and removes maintenance on a property that is now larger than necessary.

The two-step method works at every stage. What changes is how much you value the extra space and how long you expect to need it. Revisit the decision when your household changes, not just when the market does.

## How to run this comparison yourself

1. Note the house price, its property tax, insurance quote and your maintenance estimate.
2. Find two rents: a comparable house and the apartment you would actually rent.
3. Run the calculator twice, once with each rent.
4. The first run answers "own or rent?" The difference between the two runs answers "what does the extra space cost?"
5. Change the horizon. If you will stay twenty years, a house's case improves as transaction costs fade.

## The bottom line

Buying a home vs renting an apartment is rarely a like-for-like test. Renting the apartment will nearly always look cheaper, because it is less home. That does not make it the better choice for everyone. Split the decision, put a price on the space, and decide whether that space is worth it for the years you expect to need it.
