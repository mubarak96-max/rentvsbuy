## Renting vs buying a home: what is the real difference?

**Renting vs buying a home comes down to flexibility against control. Buying ties up a large amount of cash and commits you to one property, but builds equity and protects you from rent rises; renting costs less to start and leave, and lets your savings grow in other investments.** Which is better financially depends on how rents compare with prices in your area, how long you stay, and interest rates.

Rather than give one verdict, this guide compares buying vs renting a home across the ten dimensions that matter, with numbers from our calculator's late-2026 default assumptions for several countries. Use it to see where your own situation lands on each.

## The 10-dimension comparison table

| Dimension | Renting | Buying |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Upfront cash | Deposit plus first month, usually 1–3 months' rent | Down payment (5–30%) plus buying costs (1–11%) |
| 2. Month-one outflow | Rent plus small renter costs | Mortgage payment plus tax, insurance, upkeep, fees |
| 3. Cost predictability | Fixed for the lease, then rises with the market | Payment fixed (if rate fixed); repairs are lumpy |
| 4. Long-term cost trend | Rises with rent inflation | Principal and interest flat, other costs rise with value |
| 5. Wealth building | Only if you invest the savings | Forced saving through principal, plus appreciation |
| 6. Investment risk | Diversified (if invested) | Concentrated in one property, amplified by leverage |
| 7. Flexibility and exit | Leave with notice | Sell, paying 1–7% selling costs, taking weeks or months |
| 8. Maintenance | Landlord's job | Yours, typically around 1% of value a year |
| 9. Control and security | Landlord decides on renovations, pets, renewals | You decide; no one can end your occupancy |
| 10. Tax treatment | Rarely any relief | Varies: capital gains relief, some interest deductions |

The rest of this guide takes each dimension in turn.

## 1. Upfront cash: how much do you need to start?

This is the sharpest difference between renting and buying a home. A renter typically pays a security deposit and the first month's rent. A buyer pays the down payment plus buying costs.

Using our default figures:

| Market | Down payment | Buying costs | Total cash to buy | Monthly rent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| United States | US$84,000 (20%) | US$12,600 (3%) | US$96,600 | US$2,100 |
| United Kingdom | £29,000 (10%) | £7,250 (2.5%) | £36,250 | £1,350 |
| Canada | C$140,000 (20%) | C$17,500 (2.5%) | C$157,500 | C$2,300 |
| Germany | €90,000 (20%) | €45,000 (10%) | €135,000 | €1,500 |
| UAE (Dubai) | AED 360,000 (20%) | AED 126,000 (7%) | AED 486,000 | AED 10,000 |

Smaller deposits are possible in many countries, but usually come with mortgage insurance. In Canada, for example, Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation (CMHC) insurance is required below 20% down, with premiums that rise as the deposit shrinks. In the UK, the default buyer pays no Stamp Duty Land Tax: first-time buyers in England pay nothing on the first £300,000, according to [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/stamp-duty-land-tax/residential-property-rates).

## 2. Month-one outflow: what you pay each month at the start

A mortgage payment is only part of what owners pay. Here is the first month for each side:

| Market | Rent (incl. renter costs) | Mortgage payment | Other owner costs | Total owner outflow |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| United States | US$2,115 | US$2,080 | US$928 | US$3,007 |
| United Kingdom | £1,360 | £1,421 | £278 | £1,699 |
| Canada | C$2,325 | C$3,081 | C$1,167 | C$4,248 |
| Australia | A$2,815 | A$4,133 | A$1,125 | A$5,258 |

In every one of these markets the owner pays more at the start, from about 25% more in the UK to around 85% more in Canada and Australia. That gap is the money a renter can invest. Whether it is worth paying depends on what comes back through equity and appreciation.

## 3. Cost predictability

Renters know their cost for the length of the lease, then face whatever the market brings at renewal. Owners with a fixed-rate mortgage know their principal and interest for the fixed period, which can be the full term in the US or France, but is often only 1–5 years in Canada, the UK and Australia. Owners also face irregular bills: a roof, a boiler, a strata special levy. Renters get a ceiling on surprises; owners get a ceiling on the biggest line item, at least while the rate is fixed.

## 4. How costs change over ten years

Rent tends to rise every year. Mortgage principal and interest does not (on a fixed rate). By year ten, using US defaults, rent has grown from US$2,100 to about US$2,860 a month, while the mortgage payment is still US$2,080. Property tax, maintenance and insurance rise with the home's value, from about US$928 to about US$1,308 a month.

This is why buying vs renting a home often looks bad in year one and better in year ten. The longer you hold, the more rent growth works in the owner's favour. Our article on [whether it is cheaper to rent or buy](/blog/is-it-cheaper-to-rent-or-buy/) walks through that month-one versus year-ten shift in detail.

## 5. Wealth building: equity vs an investment portfolio

Owners build wealth in two ways: paying down principal (in the US example, about US$52,600 over ten years) and appreciation (a US$420,000 home growing 3.5% a year is worth about US$592,000 after ten years). Renters build wealth only by investing what they did not spend on buying.

When both sides do their part, the results over ten years with our defaults look like this:

| Market | Buyer net worth | Renter net worth | Ahead |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States | US$273,500 | US$315,300 | Renter |
| United Kingdom | £196,700 | £90,600 | Buyer |
| Canada | C$488,300 | C$585,800 | Renter |
| Australia | A$833,400 | A$816,400 | Buyer (narrowly) |
| Spain | €238,700 | €164,900 | Buyer |

Buyer net worth is home value minus selling costs and the remaining mortgage. Renter net worth is the invested down payment, buying costs and monthly savings. See [renting and investing vs buying a house](/blog/renting-and-investing-vs-buying-a-house/) for why the renter's number depends heavily on discipline.

## 6. Investment risk and leverage

A buyer with 20% down has five times leverage on the home's value. If prices rise 10%, the equity rises 50%. If prices fall 10%, half the deposit is gone. Australia shows how sensitive this is: with our defaults, the buyer is about A$17,000 ahead after ten years at 5% annual price growth, but about A$103,000 behind at 4% growth. A renter with a diversified fund holds less concentrated risk, though stock markets can fall further and faster than house prices.

## 7. Flexibility and the cost of leaving

A renter can leave at the end of the lease. An owner has to sell, which costs money and time. Our defaults use selling costs of about 6% in the US, 5% in Canada, 2% in the UK and 2.5% in Australia. Add buying costs and a round trip costs somewhere between about 4.5% (UK) and 15% (Spain) of the price. That is why short stays almost always favour renting. Our [break-even point guide](/blog/rent-vs-buy-break-even-point/) shows how many years each market needs.

## 8. Maintenance responsibility

Owners pay for everything that breaks. A common rule of thumb is around 1% of the home's value a year, more for older houses and less for new apartments where a service charge covers the building. Renters may pay for minor wear but not for structural repairs. If you rent, you can treat that difference as part of what your rent buys.

## 9. Control and security

Owners decide on renovations, pets, decorating and how long they stay. Renters depend on the landlord and on local tenancy law, which varies widely. For some households, especially those with children in school, this dimension outweighs the financial result. Our guide to [owning a home vs renting](/blog/owning-a-home-vs-renting/) looks at the non-financial side in depth.

## 10. Tax treatment

Tax rules differ by country and are not modelled in our calculator. Some examples:

- **United States:** Mortgage interest is deductible only if you itemize, which is less common since the standard deduction was raised.
- **India:** Under the old tax regime, interest on a self-occupied home loan can be deducted up to ₹2 lakh a year under Section 24(b); this is not available under the new regime.
- **Ireland:** Stamp duty is 1% on residential property up to €1 million, according to [Revenue](https://www.revenue.ie/en/property/stamp-duty/property/stamp-duty-property/rates.aspx).
- **Many countries:** A gain on your main home is often exempt from capital gains tax, which helps owners compared with renters whose investments may be taxed.

## How to weigh the 10 dimensions for yourself

1. Score the financial dimensions with numbers: run your own figures in the [rent vs own calculator](/rent-vs-own-calculator/) to see the gap in net worth.
2. Decide how many years you will stay. If it is under five, dimension 7 usually decides it.
3. Check your cash buffer after the down payment. If it is thin, dimension 3 (lumpy costs) is a real risk.
4. Put a monthly value on control and security. If buying costs you an extra US$300 a month but that stability is worth more to you, buying is a fair choice.
5. Revisit when rates or rents change, as both shift the balance.
