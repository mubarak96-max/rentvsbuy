## Should I rent or buy a house? Start with these four questions

**You should lean toward buying if you plan to stay at least five to seven years, your income is stable, you can pay the down payment and buying costs and still keep an emergency fund, and local rents are at or above the break-even rent for your market. If any one of those fails, especially the time horizon or the cash buffer, renting is usually the safer choice for now.**

General guides tell you whether buying or renting is better "on average". This one is about you. Below is a 12-question checklist that turns your personal situation into a lean, followed by the numbers you need to confirm it. If you want the broad market view first, read [is it better to rent or buy a house](/blog/is-it-better-to-rent-or-buy-a-house/).

## The 12-question rent or buy checklist

Give yourself the points shown for each answer. Add them up at the end.

### Life stage and plans

1. **How long will you stay in this area?**
   - Under 3 years: −3
   - 3–5 years: −1
   - 5–10 years: +2
   - More than 10 years: +3
2. **Are big life changes likely in the next five years** (new relationship, children, moving cities, caring for parents)?
   - Very likely: −2
   - Possibly: 0
   - Unlikely: +1
3. **Would the home you can afford to buy still suit you in five years?** (enough bedrooms, commute, schools)
   - No: −2
   - Probably: +1
   - Yes: +2

### Job and income stability

4. **How secure is your income?**
   - Contract, commission or probation: −2
   - Fairly secure: +1
   - Very secure (or two stable incomes): +2
5. **Could your job require you to relocate?**
   - Yes, likely: −2
   - Unlikely: +1
6. **Would the total monthly cost of owning be under about 35% of your take-home pay?** (include mortgage, property tax, insurance, upkeep and any service charge)
   - No: −3
   - Yes: +2

### Savings and money habits

7. **After paying the down payment and buying costs, would you still have three to six months of expenses saved?**
   - No: −3
   - Yes: +2
8. **Do you carry high-interest debt, such as credit cards?**
   - Yes: −2
   - No: +1
9. **If you rent, would you reliably invest the money you would otherwise put into a home?**
   - Yes, automatically every month: −1
   - Probably not: +2

### The local market

10. **How does local rent compare with the price of a similar home?** Divide the price by one year's rent.
    - More than 25: −2
    - 15–25: 0
    - Under 15: +2
11. **What are buying and selling costs where you live?**
    - Over 10% combined (e.g. Germany, Spain, France): −1
    - 5–10% combined: 0
    - Under 5% combined (e.g. UK first-time buyer, New Zealand): +1
12. **How much do you value control and stability** (renovating, pets, no landlord, school continuity)?
    - Low: 0
    - Medium: +1
    - High: +2

### What your score means

| Total score | Lean | What to do next |
|---|---|---|
| −10 or lower | Strongly rent | Keep renting; build savings and revisit in a year |
| −9 to −1 | Lean rent | Run the numbers; buying likely costs you money now |
| 0 to +7 | Toss-up | The financial math decides; check break-even rent and year |
| +8 or higher | Lean buy | Confirm affordability under higher rates, then shop |

The checklist is a structured gut check, not a verdict. Its job is to flag the personal factors, such as a short horizon or no emergency fund, that can make buying risky even when the market math looks good.

## Why time horizon carries the most weight

Buying costs money on day one (stamp duty, transfer tax, legal fees, lender fees) and on the day you sell (agent commission and legal fees). Those costs are fixed whether you stay two years or twenty. A short stay concentrates them; a long stay dilutes them.

Our calculator shows this clearly through the **break-even rent**, which is the monthly rent at which renting and buying leave you equally well off at the end of your horizon. Using our US defaults (a US$420,000 home with 20% down at 6.3%):

| How long you stay | Break-even rent | Typical rent | Lean |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 years | ~US$3,040 | US$2,100 | Rent |
| 5 years | ~US$2,620 | US$2,100 | Rent |
| 7 years | ~US$2,440 | US$2,100 | Rent |
| 10 years | ~US$2,310 | US$2,100 | Rent (narrowly) |
| 20 years | ~US$2,180 | US$2,100 | Rent (very narrowly) |

Notice how the threshold falls as the horizon lengthens. A longer stay makes buying competitive at lower rents. In markets where rents are high relative to prices, the same effect shows up as an early **break-even year**: with our defaults, about two years in the UK, three in Dubai, four in London and Singapore, and five in Spain. More on this in the [rent vs buy break-even guide](/blog/rent-vs-buy-break-even-point/).

## Four example households

These examples use our calculator's default assumptions for each market and a 10-year horizon.

**Tom, 29, in the UK, buying a £290,000 home with 10% down.** He plans to stay ten years and has a secure NHS job. His mortgage payment would be about £1,421 against rent of £1,350, and as a first-time buyer he pays no Stamp Duty below £300,000. With these defaults, buying leaves him about £106,000 better off after ten years. Checklist and math agree: buy.

**Sarah, 34, in Toronto, looking at a C$1.05 million home.** She would pay about C$4,620 a month in mortgage payments alone, against C$2,700 rent for a similar place. With Toronto defaults, renting and investing leaves her about C$364,000 ahead after ten years. Unless she can buy for much less or rent rises sharply, she should rent.

**Ahmed, 38, in Dubai, with a stable job and family settled in the city.** A AED 1.8 million apartment costs about AED 8,000 a month in mortgage payments plus AED 1,500 in service charges, against AED 10,000 rent. Despite roughly 7% buying costs, buying pulls ahead in about three years with Dubai defaults. If his visa and job are secure, buying makes sense.

**Priya, 31, in an Indian metro, considering an ₹80 lakh flat.** Her EMI would be about ₹55,500 against rent of ₹30,000, and Indian rental yields are low. With India defaults, renting and investing leaves her about ₹13 lakh ahead after ten years, and buying does not pull ahead even over 30 years at default prices. She should rent and invest, unless she values ownership highly or finds a much better price-to-rent deal.

## Should I buy a home or rent while I'm saving?

If your score is close to zero and your deposit is still growing, renting while you save is not wasted time. Keep the deposit in something low-risk if you plan to buy within a few years, because a market fall right before purchase can set you back. If buying is further off, consider whether your savings can earn more in a diversified portfolio. Our guide on the [opportunity cost of a down payment](/blog/opportunity-cost-of-a-down-payment/) explains this trade-off.

## Red flags that mean "not yet"

Even with a high score, hold off if:

- You would need to borrow the maximum the bank offers.
- You have not checked how the payment changes if your rate rises by two points when it resets.
- You have not budgeted for maintenance, typically around 1% of the home's value each year.
- You are buying mainly because rent "feels like throwing money away".

That last one is worth unpacking. Owners also have costs they never get back: interest, property tax, upkeep and transaction costs. Our article on [whether a mortgage is cheaper than rent](/blog/is-a-mortgage-cheaper-than-rent/) shows why the payment comparison can mislead.

## How to confirm your answer with real numbers

1. Find the asking price of a home you would buy and the rent on a similar one nearby.
2. Open the [rent vs buy calculator for your country](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/) so local defaults load.
3. Enter your real price, rent, deposit, mortgage rate and horizon.
4. Look at the break-even rent: if your rent is below it, renting is ahead.
5. Look at the break-even year: if it falls after your planned move date, renting is ahead.
6. Stress-test with one point less appreciation and one point higher interest.

If the checklist and the numbers point the same way, you have your answer. If they disagree, the numbers tell you the price of following your preference, and you can decide whether it is worth paying. This is general guidance, not personal financial advice.
