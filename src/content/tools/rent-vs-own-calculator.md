## Is it better to rent or own?

**With typical US numbers in late 2026, renting a comparable home and investing the difference leaves you slightly wealthier than owning over 10 years, by roughly $40,000 on a $420,000 home.** That gap is small enough to flip. Owning wins if rent is above about $2,310 a month for the same home, if prices rise faster than 3.5% a year, or if you can't reliably invest what renting saves you. This rent vs own calculator lets you test those conditions with your own numbers rather than a national average.

The question is not "Is a mortgage cheaper than rent?" It is "Which path leaves me with more net worth by the time I would move or sell?" The calculator answers that by running both paths month by month.

## How to use this rent vs own calculator

Start with the four inputs that move the result most:

1. **Home price and comparable rent.** Use a real listing and the rent for a similar home in the same area. Don't compare a three-bed house you'd buy with a one-bed you rent now.
2. **Horizon.** Set this to how long you realistically expect to stay. Buying and selling costs are fixed per transaction, so a short stay makes them weigh more.
3. **Mortgage rate.** Use the rate you've actually been quoted. The 6.3% default sits below the 7.18% average that Freddie Mac's [Primary Mortgage Market Survey](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms) reported for 5 November 2026, so most buyers today should raise it.
4. **Appreciation and investment return.** These are the two numbers nobody knows. Try a pessimistic and an optimistic case, not just the defaults.

The remaining inputs are property tax, maintenance, insurance, HOA fees, closing costs and rent growth. They matter, but they're easier to look up. US property tax ranges from roughly 0.3% of value in Hawaii to over 2% in New Jersey and Illinois, so replace the 1.1% default with your county's rate.

### Why we chose these US defaults

The defaults describe a typical owner-occupied home bought with 20% down: a $420,000 price, $2,100 rent, a 30-year fixed loan, 3% closing costs, 6% selling costs, 1% a year for maintenance, and 3.5% annual growth in both prices and rents. The 7% investment return is meant to be an after-tax, long-run figure for a diversified stock portfolio. None of these is a forecast. They're a sensible starting point you should edit.

## Worked example: owning vs renting with the defaults

**Buying.** A 20% down payment on $420,000 is $84,000, and 3% closing costs add $12,600, so you need **$96,600** in cash on day one. The remaining $336,000 is borrowed at 6.3% over 30 years. Using the standard amortization formula, payment = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), where r = 0.063 ÷ 12 and n = 360. That gives a monthly payment of about **$2,080**.

On top of the mortgage, you pay property tax of about $385 a month, maintenance of about $350 and insurance of about $193. Your first month of ownership costs roughly **$3,007**.

**Renting.** Rent is $2,100 plus $15 for renter's insurance, so **$2,115**. The renter puts the $96,600 into investments on day one and invests the roughly $890 a month that renting saves.

**After 10 years.** The home is worth about $592,000 and the loan balance is about $283,000. After 6% selling costs, the owner's equity is roughly $273,500. The renter's portfolio is about $315,000. **Renting leads by roughly $41,000.**

The headline numbers are less useful than the two outputs below them:

- **Break-even rent: about $2,310 a month.** If a comparable home rents for less than this, renting wins over 10 years. If it rents for more, owning wins.
- **Break-even year: none within 30 years**, on these exact assumptions. Small changes, covered below, bring it within reach.

## How does the time you stay change the answer?

How long you stay is the input people underrate most. The table below keeps every default the same and changes only the horizon.

| Years you stay | Owner net worth | Renter net worth | Leader | Break-even rent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | ~$114,000 | ~$153,000 | Rent by ~$39k | ~$3,040 |
| 5 | ~$155,000 | ~$194,000 | Rent by ~$39k | ~$2,620 |
| 7 | ~$200,000 | ~$240,000 | Rent by ~$40k | ~$2,440 |
| 10 | ~$274,000 | ~$315,000 | Rent by ~$41k | ~$2,310 |
| 15 | ~$420,000 | ~$464,000 | Rent by ~$44k | ~$2,220 |
| 20 | ~$605,000 | ~$653,000 | Rent by ~$49k | ~$2,170 |

Look at the last column. The rent that makes owning worthwhile falls the longer you stay, from over $3,000 for a three-year stay to about $2,170 over 20 years. A longer stay spreads closing and selling costs over more years, and more of each payment goes to principal. Owning gets more competitive with time even when, as here, it never quite takes the lead.

## What makes owning win instead of renting?

These are single changes from the defaults, with the 10-year outcome for each.

| Change from defaults | 10-year result |
|---|---|
| Home price $350,000 (same rent) | Owning ahead by ~$35,000; break-even in year 6 |
| Rent $2,500 instead of $2,100 | Owning ahead by ~$37,000; break-even in year 7 |
| Appreciation 4.5% instead of 3.5% | Owning ahead by ~$6,000 |
| Investment return 5% instead of 7% | Owning ahead by ~$5,000 |
| Mortgage rate 5.5% instead of 6.3% | Renting ahead by ~$6,000 (near even) |
| Mortgage rate 7.18% (Nov 2026 average) | Renting ahead by ~$81,000 |
| Property tax 2% instead of 1.1% | Renting ahead by ~$104,000 |
| Appreciation 2.5% instead of 3.5% | Renting ahead by ~$85,000 |

Two patterns stand out. The **price-to-rent ratio** is the single strongest signal. The price-to-rent ratio is the home price divided by a year's rent, and here it is 16.7. Bring it down to about 14 with a $350,000 home and owning comes out ahead. Second, **rates and property tax matter more than most people expect**. A 2% property tax state can turn a close call into a clear win for renting.

## What owning gives you that a calculator can't price

A rent vs own comparison measures money. Some of the strongest reasons to own aren't financial, and some of the strongest reasons to rent aren't either.

**Owning gives you:**
- Control over the home: renovations, pets, decor, no landlord inspections.
- Protection from rent increases and non-renewal, since a fixed-rate payment never rises.
- Forced saving through principal repayment, which helps if you wouldn't invest otherwise.
- A leveraged asset. A 10% rise in a home's value is a 50% gain on a 20% down payment.

**Renting gives you:**
- Flexibility to move for work, family or a cheaper area without a 6% selling bill.
- Diversification. Your wealth isn't tied to one property in one neighborhood.
- Predictable maintenance. The landlord pays for the roof and the furnace.
- Liquidity. An investment account can be drawn on in a week, while home equity needs a sale or a loan.

Leverage works in both directions. If prices fall in the first few years, your down payment and closing costs can be wiped out. If you're thinking about what else the money could do, read our guide to the [opportunity cost of a down payment](/blog/opportunity-cost-of-a-down-payment/).

## Owning versus renting: a decision checklist

Before you trust any rent vs own result, check these:

- [ ] **Is the rent truly comparable?** It should be the same size, area and quality as the home you'd buy.
- [ ] **Is the horizon honest?** If there's a real chance you move within five years, run a five-year case.
- [ ] **Did you use your real mortgage quote?** Rates in late 2026 are well above the 6.3% default.
- [ ] **Is property tax local?** Look up your county rate. Don't use the national default.
- [ ] **Would you really invest the difference?** If not, owning's forced saving counts for more.
- [ ] **Do you have reserves after closing?** Owning without an emergency fund turns a broken water heater into credit card debt.
- [ ] **Did you stress-test appreciation?** Run it at 2% as well as 3.5%. If owning only wins in the optimistic case, it's a lifestyle choice more than a financial one.

If owning wins under conservative assumptions, it's a strong financial case. If it only wins under optimistic ones, the decision rests on how much you value stability and control.
