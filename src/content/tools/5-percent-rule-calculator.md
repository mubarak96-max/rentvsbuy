## What is the 5% rule for renting vs buying?

**The 5% rule says the unrecoverable cost of owning a home is about 5% of its value each year, so if you can rent a comparable home for less than price × 5% ÷ 12 a month, renting is probably the better financial choice.** For a C$700,000 Canadian home that threshold is **C$2,917 a month**. At the default rent of C$2,300, the rule points to renting. This 5% rule rent vs buy calculator gives you that quick check, a version tuned to your own inputs, and the full simulation, so you can see where the rule holds and where it doesn't.

Unrecoverable costs are the money an owner spends and never gets back as equity: property tax, maintenance, insurance and the cost of capital. A renter's equivalent is simply rent. The 5% rule works by comparing like with like. It doesn't set rent against a mortgage payment, which also includes principal that you keep.

## Where the 5% rule comes from

Ben Felix of PWL Capital set out the rule in his 2019 video *Renting vs. Buying a Home: The 5% Rule*, made for Canadian investors. It has three parts:

| Part | Felix's estimate | What it represents |
|---|---|---|
| Property tax | ~1% of value | Tax paid every year by owners only |
| Maintenance | ~1% of value | Repairs, replacements, upkeep |
| Cost of capital | ~3% of value | Interest on the mortgage, plus the return your equity gives up |
| **Total** | **~5%** | **Annual unrecoverable cost** |

The 3% cost of capital comes from two numbers that happened to match in 2019. Mortgages were available at around 3%. And the expected return on stocks at the time (about 6.6%) minus the expected return on real estate (about 3%, a long-run real return of roughly 1.3% plus inflation) also came to about 3%. Because debt and equity cost about the same, the rule didn't depend much on the size of the down payment.

Felix himself called it a simplification. It's a quick check, not a full model.

## How to use the 5% rule calculator

The calculator shows three results, from simplest to most complete:

1. **Classic 5% rule:** price × 5% ÷ 12, compared with your rent.
2. **Personalised rule:** your property tax + maintenance + insurance + monthly fees (as a percentage of value) + a cost of capital worked out from your own inputs. The cost of capital is your down payment share × (investment return − appreciation) plus the borrowed share × (mortgage rate − appreciation).
3. **Full simulation:** a month-by-month comparison that includes closing costs, selling costs, rent growth and your time horizon. Its **break-even rent** is the most reliable threshold of the three.

Our personalised version differs from Felix's original in one way. It subtracts expected home price growth from **both** the mortgage rate and the investment return. In other words, it treats appreciation as offsetting the cost of all the capital tied up in the home. This gives a lower cost of capital when rates are near or below expected price growth.

## Worked example: the 5% rule in Canada

The Canadian defaults are a **C$700,000 home**, **C$2,300 rent**, a 4.4% mortgage over 25 years, 20% down, 0.8% property tax, 1% maintenance, 0.2% insurance, 3% appreciation and a 6.5% investment return.

**Classic rule.** C$700,000 × 5% = C$35,000 a year, or **C$2,917 a month**. Rent is C$2,300, so **the rule says rent**.

**Personalised rule.** Property tax, maintenance and insurance add up to 2.0%. Cost of capital = 0.2 × (6.5% − 3%) + 0.8 × (4.4% − 3%) = 0.70% + 1.12% = 1.82%. The total is **3.82%**, or about **C$2,228 a month**. Rent is C$2,300, so the personalised rule leans slightly towards **buying**.

**Full simulation over 10 years.** The C$560,000 mortgage costs about C$3,081 a month on the calculator's standard monthly-compounding formula. (Canadian fixed-rate mortgages compound semi-annually, which gives about C$3,068.) With tax, maintenance and insurance, owning costs about C$4,248 a month at first, against C$2,325 for renting. After 10 years, **renting leads by roughly C$97,000**, and the **break-even rent is about C$2,804**.

So who's right? The classic rule is. The personalised rule leaves out **transaction costs**: 2.5% to buy and 5% to sell, or about C$65,000 of costs on this home over the horizon. Those costs are what push the true threshold up from C$2,228 to C$2,804. On these numbers, the classic 5% figure of C$2,917 happens to land close to the full answer.

Check: at a rent of C$2,917, the simulation has buying ahead by about C$22,000 after 10 years, with buying pulling ahead in year 8.

## How to customise the 5% rule for your market

Replace each part with a local figure:

- **Property tax:** use your municipality's actual rate. Canadian rates vary widely between cities. Many US states sit between 1% and 2%, while the UK has no recurring owner-only property tax, because council tax is paid by renters too.
- **Maintenance:** 1% is a reasonable average. Use 1.5% to 2% for older detached houses, and less for new builds.
- **Condo or HOA fees:** convert to a percentage of the price (monthly fee × 12 ÷ price) and add it.
- **Cost of capital:** recalculate it from your mortgage rate and expected returns. Don't keep 3% when rates have moved.
- **Transaction costs:** if you might move within about seven years, add (buying % + selling %) ÷ years you'll stay.

Toronto shows the rule at its most useful. A C$1,050,000 home renting for C$2,700 has a price-to-rent ratio above 32. Even the classic rule's C$4,375 threshold is far above rent, and the full simulation has renting ahead by roughly C$364,000 over 10 years.

## When does the answer flip in Canada?

The full simulation's break-even rent shifts with your horizon and assumptions. The classic 5% threshold doesn't move.

| Canadian scenario | Break-even rent | 5% rule threshold |
|---|---|---|
| 5-year stay | ~C$3,180 | C$2,917 |
| 10-year stay (default) | ~C$2,804 | C$2,917 |
| 20-year stay | ~C$2,689 | C$2,917 |
| 10 years, 4% appreciation | ~C$2,386 | C$2,917 |

Over a short stay, the 5% rule is too generous to buying, because a five-year owner has to recover transaction costs over fewer years. Over a long stay, or with faster price growth, it's too harsh on buying. The rule works best as a first filter. If rent is far below the threshold, renting is very likely the better choice. If rent is far above, buying probably is. Anything close to the threshold needs the full calculation.

## Limitations of the 5% rule

- **It ignores how long you stay.** Buying and selling costs make short stays expensive, and the rule takes no account of them.
- **It's a snapshot.** Rent tends to rise over time, while a fixed mortgage payment doesn't. That favours long-term owners in a way the rule can't capture.
- **It assumes the renter invests the difference.** If you wouldn't, the rule overstates the case for renting.
- **It's sensitive to the cost of capital.** One percentage point changes the threshold by C$583 a month on a C$700,000 home.
- **It leaves out taxes.** In Canada, gains on a principal residence are generally tax-free under the principal residence exemption, while non-registered investment returns are taxed. Use an after-tax return.
- **Canadian mortgage terms renew.** Your rate resets every one to five years, so today's rate may not apply for the whole 25-year amortization.
- **Down payments under 20% need CMHC insurance**, which the rule doesn't include.

When the classic rule, the personalised rule and the full simulation all give the same answer, you can be fairly confident. When they disagree, as in the Canadian example, trust the full simulation and look at which input is causing the difference.
