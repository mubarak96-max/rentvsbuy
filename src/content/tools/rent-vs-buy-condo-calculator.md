## Is it better to buy a condo or rent?

**In high-cost cities with large monthly fees, renting a comparable apartment is usually the better financial choice. Buying a condo tends to win only when fees are modest, the price is low relative to rent, and you stay for many years.** On our New York City defaults, an $850,000 condo with $1,200 a month in common charges leaves the buyer roughly $247,000 behind a renter after 10 years. Remove those charges and the gap almost disappears. This rent vs buy condo calculator puts the fees at the centre of the comparison, where they belong.

Condo fees are the monthly charges owners pay the building's association for shared costs and reserves. Depending on the country they're called HOA dues, common charges, strata fees or service charges. They behave like a second rent that never builds equity, and they usually rise over time. The calculator increases them each year in line with rent growth.

## How to use the rent vs buy condo calculator

1. **Price and comparable rent.** Use rents for similar units in the same building or on the same block.
2. **Monthly fees.** Enter the current common charges from the listing or the offering plan. For a co-op, enter the full maintenance charge and set property tax to 0%.
3. **Property tax.** For condos, use the unit's actual tax bill as a percentage of the price. Some new NYC developments have tax abatements that later expire. If so, model the full tax.
4. **Maintenance.** Keep this lower than for a house, because the building handles exterior and structural work. Our NYC preset uses 0.5% of value for your unit's interior.
5. **Appreciation.** Be cautious. Try one percentage point below what you'd assume for a house.
6. **Buying and selling costs.** The NYC preset uses 4% to buy and 7% to sell, because city and state transfer taxes add to standard fees.

## Worked example: buying vs renting an $850,000 NYC condo

**Upfront.** A 20% down payment of $170,000 plus 4% closing costs of $34,000 comes to **$204,000**.

**Mortgage.** A $680,000 loan at 6.3% over 30 years. The amortization formula, with a monthly rate of 0.525% over 360 months, gives about **$4,209** a month.

**Monthly cost to own versus rent:**

| Monthly, year one | Condo owner | Renter |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage (principal + interest) | $4,209 | — |
| Common charges | $1,200 | — |
| Property tax (0.9%) | ~$638 | — |
| Unit maintenance (0.5%) | ~$354 | — |
| Insurance (0.2%) | ~$142 | — |
| Rent | — | $4,200 |
| Renter's insurance | — | $20 |
| **Total** | **~$6,542** | **~$4,220** |

The common charges alone make up almost a fifth of the owner's monthly bill. Over 10 years, rising at 4% a year, they add up to about **$173,000**, none of which builds equity.

**After 10 years.** The condo is worth about $1.14 million, with about $574,000 left on the loan. After 7% selling costs, the owner keeps equity of roughly **$489,000**. The renter, who invested the $204,000 upfront and the monthly difference, has about **$736,000**. **Renting is ahead by roughly $247,000.** The **break-even rent is about $5,430**: buying wins only if a comparable unit rents for more than that.

## How much do condo fees change the answer?

This is the most important table on the page. Every input except the monthly fees stays at the NYC defaults, and the horizon is 10 years.

| Monthly common charges | 10-year result | Break-even rent |
|---|---|---|
| $0 | Renting ahead by ~$6,000 (buying pulls ahead in year 11) | ~$4,230 |
| $800 | Renting ahead by ~$167,000 | ~$5,030 |
| $1,200 (default) | Renting ahead by ~$247,000 | ~$5,430 |
| $1,600 | Renting ahead by ~$328,000 | ~$5,830 |

**On these assumptions, every $100 a month of condo fees costs about $20,000 of net worth over 10 years** once you count the investment return that money would have earned. It also raises the break-even rent by about $100. When you compare two listings, a cheaper unit with higher fees can easily be the more expensive one to own.

## Other condo-specific risks

**Special assessments.** If the building's reserves are thin, owners pay for major repairs directly. Ask for the reserve study, the past two years of board minutes and any planned capital projects. A building that has kept fees artificially low may be heading for an assessment.

**Slower appreciation.** A condo owner holds a small slice of the land, competes with new towers nearby and depends on how well the building is run. Here's what happens with different appreciation rates on the NYC defaults over 10 years:

| Appreciation per year | 10-year result |
|---|---|
| 2% | Renting ahead by ~$336,000 |
| 3% (default) | Renting ahead by ~$247,000 |
| 4% | Renting ahead by ~$150,000 |

**Rules and restrictions.** Condo bylaws can limit renting out your unit, renovations and pets. That affects both how you can live in it and your options if you move away.

**Rent level.** The renter's advantage shrinks as comparable rent rises. At $5,000 a month instead of $4,200, renting's 10-year lead drops to about $86,000, and buying pulls ahead in year 19.

**Time doesn't rescue a high-fee condo.** For many homes, staying longer makes buying look better. Here, renting's lead keeps growing, because the common charges rise every year along with rent:

| Years you own | Renting ahead by | Break-even rent |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | ~$159,000 | ~$6,280 |
| 10 | ~$247,000 | ~$5,430 |
| 15 | ~$363,000 | ~$5,120 |
| 20 | ~$514,000 | ~$4,940 |

The break-even rent does come down the longer you stay, so a cheaper condo or a pricier comparable rental gets closer to the line over time. But at $4,200 rent and $1,200 a month in fees, this condo doesn't break even within 20 years.

## Co-op vs condo: what changes in the calculation

| | Condo | Co-op |
|---|---|---|
| What you own | The unit (real property) | Shares in the building corporation |
| Monthly charge | Common charges; property tax billed separately | Maintenance that includes property tax and any building mortgage |
| How to enter it | Fees + property tax % | Full maintenance in fees, property tax 0% |
| Buyer approval | Usually only a right of first refusal | Board approval; can reject applicants |
| Down payment | Set by your lender | Often a higher minimum set by the board |
| On sale | Standard transfer taxes | Transfer taxes, plus a flip tax in many buildings |
| Subletting | Usually more flexible | Often restricted |

As a quick test, take the default condo and enter it as a co-op: property tax at 0% and maintenance of $1,900 a month (the $1,200 common charges plus roughly the $638 tax, rounded up). Renting is ahead by about $263,000 over 10 years, which is in the same range. The structure matters less than the total monthly cost.

## New York transaction costs to know

According to the [New York State Department of Taxation and Finance](https://www.tax.ny.gov/bus/transfer/rptidx.htm), the state real estate transfer tax is $2 per $500 of consideration, which works out to 0.4%, and it's generally paid by the seller. Buyers of residential property costing **$1 million or more** also pay the **1% "mansion tax"**. Sellers also pay New York City's Real Property Transfer Tax, and buyers who take out a mortgage on a condo pay the mortgage recording tax. Co-op share loans generally aren't subject to recording tax, because they aren't mortgages on real property. An $850,000 purchase stays under the mansion tax threshold. At $1 million or more, raise the buying costs input.

## Condo vs renting: decision checklist

- [ ] Have you entered the **actual** common charges, and checked how much they've risen in recent years?
- [ ] Have you read the **reserve study and board minutes** for planned work and past assessments?
- [ ] Is any **tax abatement** expiring during your horizon?
- [ ] Have you tested **appreciation at 2%**?
- [ ] Is your **comparable rent** for the same size and quality of unit?
- [ ] Do the **bylaws** allow you to rent the unit out if you move?
- [ ] For a co-op: what's the **flip tax**, the **minimum down payment** and the **sublet policy**?
