## How do you use the rent vs buy calculator for the US?

**Enter the price of the home you would actually buy, the rent on a comparable home, your mortgage rate and your state's property tax rate — those four inputs decide most US rent vs buy answers.** With our national defaults, renting and investing the difference finishes ahead of buying over 10 years, and buying only wins if a comparable rental costs more than roughly $2,300 a month.

This rent vs buy calculator for the USA runs a month-by-month simulation. The buyer pays the down payment and closing costs upfront, then a standard amortizing mortgage, property tax, insurance and maintenance, and receives the sale proceeds minus selling costs at the end. The renter pays rent and renters insurance, invests the cash the buyer spent upfront, and each month whoever has the lower housing bill invests the difference. That "same cash flow" approach is explained in full on our [methodology page](/methodology/).

Our 6.3% default is deliberately a mid-cycle figure so the page does not swing every week, but if you are buying now, replace it with your actual quote. The PMMS average assumes a borrower with strong credit and 20% down, so your rate may be higher.

## Worked example: renting vs buying a $420,000 US home

Here is what the calculator does with the defaults.

**Upfront cash for the buyer:** $84,000 down payment plus $12,600 closing costs = **$96,600**. The renter keeps this and invests it.

**Monthly mortgage payment:** A $336,000 loan at 6.3% over 30 years costs **$2,080 a month** in principal and interest, using the standard amortization formula M = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), where r is the monthly rate (0.525%) and n is 360 payments.

**Other owner costs in month one:** property tax ($385), maintenance ($350) and homeowners insurance ($193) add about **$928 a month**. Total owner outflow: roughly **$3,008 a month** against **$2,115** for the renter (rent plus $15 renters insurance). The renter invests the ~$890 gap every month.

**Result after 10 years:** the renter's portfolio finishes roughly $40,000 ahead of the buyer's home equity after a 6% selling cost. The **break-even rent** is about $2,300: if you can rent the same home for less than that, renting wins on these assumptions.

**What flips it:** at today's 7.18% PMMS rate, the payment rises to about $2,276 and renting's lead roughly doubles. In the other direction, cutting the selling cost to 5% or living in a state with 0.5% property tax narrows the gap considerably. The [5% rule](/5-percent-rule-calculator/) gives the same direction at a glance: $420,000 × 5% ÷ 12 = $1,750, and since rent ($2,100) is above that, buying looks reasonable on the quick test — but the full simulation, with 20% down tying up capital at a 7% return, is stricter.

### PMI: the cost of putting less than 20% down

Private mortgage insurance protects the lender, not you. On a conventional loan it can usually be cancelled once you reach 20% equity and drops off automatically at 22% under the federal Homeowners Protection Act. If you put 10% down on the default home, your loan is $378,000, the payment rises to about $2,340, and PMI adds to that. Model it by raising the insurance input until you expect to hit 20% equity. FHA loans charge mortgage insurance differently and often for the life of the loan.

### Selling costs after the 2024 NAR settlement

Since August 17, 2024, listings on MLS services can no longer advertise compensation for buyer's agents, and buyers must sign written agreements with their agents. The change made commissions more visibly negotiable, but data from brokerages and industry trackers through 2026 suggests total commissions have fallen only modestly, if at all, and many sellers still offer to pay the buyer's agent. That is why we keep 6% as the default selling cost. If you plan to use a flat-fee or discount broker, try 4–5% and watch how much the break-even year moves.

## How do taxes change the US rent vs buy decision?

**Property tax is the biggest tax variable.** Effective rates range from about 0.3% of value in Hawaii to more than 2% in New Jersey and Illinois. On a $420,000 home that is the difference between roughly $1,300 and $8,400 a year — enough on its own to change the verdict. Texas has no state income tax but high property taxes; California's Proposition 13 keeps tax tied to purchase price, so long-time owners pay low effective rates while new buyers pay around 1.1–1.25% of what they paid.

**The mortgage interest deduction helps fewer buyers than people think.** Interest on up to $750,000 of acquisition debt is deductible only if you itemize. With the 2026 standard deduction at about $32,200 for joint filers, a couple paying $21,000 of first-year interest and $4,600 of property tax on the default home would still take the standard deduction unless they have large state income taxes or charitable gifts. The cap on deducting state and local taxes (SALT) was raised to $40,000 for 2025 and rises slightly in later years, with a phase-down for higher incomes, which makes itemizing more common in high-tax states. Mortgage insurance premiums also became deductible again from 2026 for itemizers, subject to income limits. Our calculator ignores all of this, so treat it as a conservative estimate for buyers who itemize.

**Capital gains:** married couples can exclude up to $500,000 of gain on a primary residence ($250,000 if single) after living there two of the last five years. Most buyers will not owe tax on the sale, which keeps the comparison simple.

## How do inputs change across US cities?

The national defaults hide huge regional differences. A few reference points:

- **New York City:** prices and rents are far higher, co-op maintenance or condo common charges are significant, and buyers face mansion tax above $1 million. The New York City preset on our [country and city hub](/rent-vs-buy-calculator/) starts around $850,000 with $1,200 a month in fees. Renting usually wins over 10 years.
- **San Francisco Bay Area and Los Angeles:** very high price-to-rent ratios (often 25–35) mean renting and investing typically wins unless you stay 15 years or more.
- **Austin and Dallas:** moderate prices but property taxes near 2% in many Texas counties. Raise the property tax input before trusting the result.
- **Chicago:** Cook County effective tax rates are among the highest in the country, while prices are moderate; the result is often close.
- **Cleveland, Pittsburgh, Memphis:** lower price-to-rent ratios (often under 15). Buying tends to break even within five to seven years.

To compare a specific metro, divide the home price by annual rent. That is the [price-to-rent ratio](/blog/price-to-rent-ratio/): under 15 tends to favour buying, over 20 tends to favour renting.

## When does renting win, and when does buying win, in the US?

**Renting tends to win when:**

- You expect to move within five to seven years, because 3% to buy and ~6% to sell take years to recover.
- The price-to-rent ratio is above about 20, which is common in coastal cities.
- Your mortgage rate is near or above 7% and you would invest the savings consistently.
- You live in a high property tax county and will not itemize.

**Buying tends to win when:**

- You plan to stay 10 years or more and value stability.
- Local rents are high relative to prices, or rents are rising faster than 3.5% a year.
- You can lock a fixed rate you are comfortable with, knowing you can refinance if rates fall — a one-way option renters do not have.
- You would not otherwise invest the down payment and monthly difference; forced saving through principal repayment is real.

## US rent vs buy checklist

Before you rely on the result, work through these:

1. **Replace the rate** with a real quote, not the PMMS average.
2. **Look up the effective property tax** on a comparable home in your county.
3. **Add PMI** to insurance if you are putting down less than 20%.
4. **Add HOA dues** if you are buying a condo or a home in an association; see our [condo calculator](/rent-vs-buy-condo-calculator/).
5. **Check homeowners insurance quotes** — premiums in Florida, California and along the Gulf Coast can be several times the 0.55% default.
6. **Set the horizon** to how long you realistically expect to stay, and look at the break-even year.
7. **Be honest about investing.** If you would spend the monthly difference, lower the investment return.
8. **Compare the break-even rent** with real listings for the same size and neighborhood.
