## How do you use the rent vs buy calculator for Ireland?

**On our Irish defaults, buying beats renting from the first year and finishes roughly €280,000 ahead after 10 years. Owning costs about the same each month as renting, while low upfront costs and steady mortgage repayments build equity.** Even with zero house-price growth, buying pulls ahead in about year five. In Ireland the hard part is rarely the maths. It is saving the deposit and getting approved under the Central Bank's lending limits.

The calculator compares two households over the horizon you choose. The buyer pays a deposit, stamp duty and legal fees, then the mortgage, Local Property Tax, maintenance and home insurance. The renter pays rent plus contents insurance and invests the deposit, plus any monthly saving, at the investment return. Both are valued at the end, after the buyer's selling costs. See our [methodology](/methodology/) for the full method.

## Worked example: a €380,000 home vs €1,900 rent

**Upfront cash:** a €38,000 deposit plus €9,500 in buying costs, or **€47,500 in total**. Stamp duty accounts for €3,800 of that.

**Monthly mortgage:** the loan is €342,000 at 3.8% over 30 years. The amortisation formula, M = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n) with r = 0.038 ÷ 12 and n = 360, gives **about €1,594 a month**. In the first month, about €1,083 is interest and €511 repays capital.

**Total monthly cost of owning in year 1:** about €1,974. That is the mortgage plus about €380 a month for LPT, maintenance and insurance. The renter pays €1,910 including contents insurance. The two are almost level, so the renter has very little to invest each month beyond the €47,500 starting pot.

**After 10 years:**

- Buyer: home worth about €619,000, minus 2% selling costs and a remaining loan of about €267,600. That gives a net worth of about **€370,000**.
- Renter: a portfolio worth about **€87,000**.
- **Break-even year: year 1.** Buying is already narrowly ahead after the first year.
- **Break-even rent:** far below any realistic Irish rent for this kind of home. You would need to rent the home for under about €450 a month for renting to win over 10 years on these assumptions.

The 5% rule says €380,000 × 5% ÷ 12 = €1,583. Rent of €1,900 sits above that, which also points to buying. Check it in the [5% rule calculator](/5-percent-rule-calculator/).

## What mortgage rules and supports apply in Ireland?

**Central Bank mortgage measures.** Borrowing is generally limited to **4× gross income** for first-time and second-time buyers, and **90% loan-to-value**. Buy-to-let loans are limited to 70% LTV. Lenders can make a limited share of loans above these limits each year as exceptions. On our defaults, a €342,000 loan needs income of about €85,500 at 4×.

**Help to Buy (HTB).** First-time buyers of a **new** home, or a self-build, can claim back income tax and DIRT paid in recent years. The refund is worth up to **€30,000 or 10% of the price**, whichever is lower, for homes priced at €500,000 or less. The scheme runs until the end of 2029, with details on [Revenue's Help to Buy pages](https://www.revenue.ie/en/property/help-to-buy-incentive/index.aspx). In the calculator, lower the deposit you fund yourself by the HTB amount.

**First Home scheme.** This shared-equity scheme can fund up to 30% of the price of a new home, or 20% if you also use HTB, in exchange for an equity stake. Price ceilings vary by county. You can redeem the stake later. The calculator does not model the stake directly. An approximate method is to treat the equity share as a lower price on entry and a smaller share of the gain on exit.

**Local Authority Home Loan.** A state-backed mortgage for buyers who cannot get enough credit from banks, subject to income limits.

## How do Irish rent rules affect the comparison?

The rental market changed on **1 March 2026**, when the Residential Tenancies (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 2026 took effect:

- **Nationwide rent controls.** The previous rent pressure zone system now effectively covers the whole country. Annual increases are capped at **CPI or 2%, whichever is lower**.
- **Tenancies of minimum duration.** New tenancies created from 1 March 2026 run for six years, on a rolling basis.
- **Rent resets.** For new tenancies, landlords can reset to market rent at the end of each six-year cycle, or when a tenant leaves voluntarily or breaches the lease.
- **Existing tenancies** continue under the earlier rules.

For a sitting tenant, the reforms make rent more predictable. Set rent growth to about 2% if you expect to stay put. For anyone moving, new-letting rents still reflect a severe shortage. That is why our default keeps 4%. The [Department of Housing's rental reform pages](https://www.gov.ie/en/department-of-housing-local-government-and-heritage/collections/rental-market-reforms-information) summarise the rules. Renters can also claim the Rent Tax Credit against income tax, which the calculator does not model.

**Local Property Tax.** LPT for 2026–2030 is based on each home's self-assessed value on 1 November 2025, using new, wider bands. Most homes pay a few hundred euro a year, and councils can vary the basic rate by a local adjustment factor.

## When does renting win in Ireland, and when does buying win?

**Renting tends to win when:**

- you expect to leave Ireland or move city within about three years
- you have an old tenancy at a rent far below market and expect it to stay capped
- buying would mean borrowing at the 4× limit with no buffer for repairs
- you are comparing a city-centre apartment with high service charges and defect risk against renting a similar unit

**Buying tends to win when:**

- you can qualify for the mortgage and stay five years or more
- your rent is at or above the full monthly cost of owning (about €1,970 on the defaults)
- you are buying a new home and can use Help to Buy or the First Home scheme
- you want security from the risk of a rent reset when your tenancy ends

## Ireland rent vs buy checklist

1. **Get an approval in principle** to confirm your 4× income limit and your actual rate.
2. **Price your deposit:** 10% of the price plus about 2.5% for stamp duty and fees.
3. **Check Help to Buy and First Home eligibility** if you are buying a new home.
4. **Set rent growth correctly:** about 2% for a protected tenancy, higher if you will move.
5. **Look up the LPT band** for the home's value.
6. **For apartments, ask for service charge accounts** and any planned levies.
7. **Stress-test at 3% price growth and a 1-point higher rate.**
8. **Compare your horizon with the break-even year.** In Ireland it is usually short.
