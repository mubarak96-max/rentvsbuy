## Is a mortgage cheaper than rent?

**A mortgage payment is often close to rent, but owning a home almost always costs more each month than the mortgage alone.** With our US defaults, the mortgage on a $420,000 home is about $2,080 a month against $2,100 rent. The true monthly cost of owning is about $3,007, and over 10 years renting comes out roughly $41,000 ahead. A rent vs mortgage calculator is only useful if it counts everything the payment leaves out. This one does.

Comparing rent to a mortgage payment is how most people start, and it's the most common mistake in the rent or buy decision. Rent is your whole housing cost. A mortgage payment isn't.

## How to use the rent vs mortgage calculator

1. **Enter the home and its comparable rent.** Use the same property, or two very similar ones.
2. **Enter your actual mortgage quote.** The 6.3% default is a long-run assumption. Freddie Mac's [Primary Mortgage Market Survey](https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms) put the 30-year fixed average at 7.18% for the week of 5 November 2026.
3. **Read the monthly payment first, then ignore it.** The monthly payment output is there so you can check it against a lender's quote. The decision rests on the net worth comparison and the break-even rent.
4. **Check total unrecoverable costs.** This output adds up everything you pay and never get back: interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance, closing costs and selling costs for the owner, and rent for the renter.

## Worked example: same payment, different outcome

**The mortgage.** A $420,000 home with 20% down leaves a $336,000 loan. At 6.3% over 30 years, the amortization formula P = L × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), with r = 0.00525 and n = 360, gives about **$2,080 a month**. That's $20 less than the $2,100 rent.

**The first month, side by side:**

| Monthly cost | Owner | Renter |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage payment (principal + interest) | $2,080 | — |
| Property tax (1.1% a year) | $385 | — |
| Home insurance (0.55% a year) | $193 | — |
| Maintenance (1% a year) | $350 | — |
| Rent | — | $2,100 |
| Renter's insurance | — | $15 |
| **Total** | **~$3,007** | **~$2,115** |

The owner pays about **$890 more** each month to start with. The calculator has the renter invest that difference, along with the $96,600 down payment and closing costs the renter didn't spend. Both households are spending exactly the same cash each month. They just put it in different places.

### Where the money goes over 10 years

| 10-year total | Owner | Renter |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage interest | ~$197,000 | — |
| Principal repaid (builds equity) | ~$53,000 | — |
| Property tax | ~$55,000 | — |
| Maintenance | ~$50,000 | — |
| Home insurance | ~$28,000 | — |
| Closing costs (3%) | ~$12,600 | — |
| Selling costs (6% of sale price) | ~$35,500 | — |
| Rent paid | — | ~$296,000 |

The owner's unrecoverable costs (everything except principal) add up to roughly **$378,000**. The renter's come to about **$298,000** including insurance. Owners do get something renters don't: the home rises in value from $420,000 to about $592,000. Renters get investment growth on a starting portfolio of nearly $97,000.

The net result after 10 years is owner equity of about $273,500 against a renter portfolio of about $315,000. **Renting leads by roughly $41,000, even though the mortgage was $20 cheaper than rent.**

## What rent equals a mortgage? Using the break-even rent

The most useful output for anyone comparing rent to a mortgage is the **break-even rent**. That's the rent at which buying and renting leave you equally well off at your horizon. With the defaults over 10 years, it's about **$2,310**.

That gives you a practical rule for this home. If you can rent something comparable for less than about $2,310, renting is the better financial choice. If comparable homes rent for more, buying is.

The break-even rent changes with the inputs that drive the result:

| Scenario (10-year horizon) | Mortgage payment | Break-even rent |
|---|---|---|
| Defaults (6.3%) | $2,080 | ~$2,310 |
| Rate 5.5% | $1,908 | ~$2,130 |
| Rate 7.18% (Nov 2026 average) | $2,276 | ~$2,510 |
| 10% down at 6.3% (no PMI) | $2,340 | ~$2,300 |
| Investment return 6% instead of 7% | $2,080 | ~$2,190 |

With 20% down and a 7% investment return, the break-even rent sits about $220 to $240 above the mortgage payment at every rate shown. Property tax, insurance and maintenance are large, but principal repayment and price growth claw much of them back. What's left is roughly that gap. The last two rows show why there's no universal rule, though. A smaller down payment raises the mortgage but leaves less money missing out on investment returns, so the break-even rent ends up slightly *below* the payment. A lower expected investment return narrows the gap to about $110.

## Rent to mortgage calculator: how much home does my rent buy?

Some people use a "rent to mortgage calculator" the other way round: "I pay $2,100 in rent. What could I afford to buy for the same money?" Here's the honest answer.

- **Principal and interest only:** at 6.3% over 30 years, $2,100 a month supports a loan of about $339,000.
- **Full ownership cost:** once you add 1.1% property tax, 0.55% insurance and 1% maintenance, the same $2,100 covers a home price of only about **$293,000** with 20% down.

That's around $130,000 less house than the payment-only figure suggests. Lenders' affordability checks include tax and insurance, but they don't include maintenance. The calculator does, and so should your budget.

## Why the monthly payment comparison misleads

**It ignores costs outside the payment.** Tax, insurance, maintenance and HOA fees can add 40% to 50% to a mortgage payment. See [hidden costs of owning a home](/blog/hidden-costs-of-owning-a-home/) for the full list.

**It ignores transaction costs.** Buying and selling a US home commonly costs around 9% of its value between them. A short stay rarely recovers that.

**It ignores opportunity cost.** A $96,600 down payment invested at 7% would grow to roughly $190,000 in 10 years. That return is a real cost of buying.

**It ignores what the payment buys you.** Early on, most of a mortgage payment is interest. Later, most of it is principal, so the same payment builds more equity each year. A fixed-rate payment also stays flat while rent typically rises.

The last point is why owning gets more competitive the longer you stay. Our guide to the [rent vs buy break-even point](/blog/rent-vs-buy-break-even-point/) goes further into timing.

## Fixed mortgage vs rising rent: how the gap closes over time

The rent vs mortgage comparison isn't fixed. A 30-year fixed payment stays at $2,080 for its whole life. Rent, property tax, insurance and maintenance all rise. Here are the yearly housing costs in the calculator with the US defaults:

| Year | Owner's housing cost that year | Renter's housing cost that year | Who spends less |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ~$36,300 | ~$25,400 | Renter by ~$10,900 |
| 5 | ~$37,900 | ~$29,100 | Renter by ~$8,800 |
| 10 | ~$40,400 | ~$34,600 | Renter by ~$5,800 |
| 15 | ~$43,300 | ~$41,100 | Renter by ~$2,200 |
| 20 | ~$46,700 | ~$48,800 | Owner by ~$2,100 |
| 30 | ~$55,600 | ~$68,800 | Owner by ~$13,200 |

Around year 18 the lines cross, and from then on the owner has spare cash to invest each month. Once the mortgage is paid off in year 31, the owner pays only tax, insurance and maintenance, while the renter's rent keeps rising. This is the strongest argument for buying if you expect to stay for decades. It's also why a short-horizon comparison of rent against a mortgage payment looks so different from a long-horizon one. Use the horizon input to see both views before you decide.

## Mortgage or rent: decision checklist

- [ ] Compare rent with the **full monthly cost of owning**, not with principal and interest.
- [ ] Use your **real rate quote**. Every 0.5% changes the payment by roughly $100 to $110 on this loan.
- [ ] Check whether comparable rent is **above or below the break-even rent**.
- [ ] Add **PMI** if you're putting down less than 20%.
- [ ] Set the horizon to **how long you'll actually stay**.
- [ ] If you'd spend rather than invest the monthly difference, read [renting and investing vs buying a house](/blog/renting-and-investing-vs-buying-a-house/).
